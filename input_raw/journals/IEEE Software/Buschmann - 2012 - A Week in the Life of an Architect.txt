Focus Is Essential
Servicing the interests of many stake-

holder groups while also driving the 
design of a system requires stringent 
focus. During the week that my diary 

sketches, I was focused on the nonfunc-

tional quality of the scenarios the walk-
ing skeleton implemented and the pres-

concepts that referenced implementa-

tion quality. Focus can differ depend-

progress, but each week, increment, or 

iteration must have a dedicated theme. 
Why? If architects, including myself,

actually achieving very little. They™re 
everywhere but not where they need to 
be.
2 Their impact is limited. Develop-
ers often don™t accept architects who 
act this way, and the more that archi-
tects™ busyness mismatches the project™s

holders tend to doubt their competence.
Although architects must address all

cess of a system™s design, they can™t seri-
ously address more than one or two of 
them at one timeŠand then only bite 

by bite. One focus per week seems like 

an appropriate bite size to me. It™s big 
enough to achieve visible progress, yet 
small enough to address all relevant as-

pects of a given project phase within a 
reasonable timeframe. Themes beyond 
this focus aren™t ignored but observed 

and added to the backlog rather than 

immediately addressedŠuntil their

Architecting by Walking Around
If you observe how pragmatic archi
-tects design, you™ll notice that they 
spend most of their time with the peo
-ple who must live with their decisions: 
project and business managers, devel
-opers, testers, and so on. They spend 
less time in meetings and more time in 
workshops that directly elaborate the 
architecture. You can see this practice 
in the diary where I was ﬁjustﬂ the ar
-chitectural mentor, not the responsible 
architect. Still, architects must allocate 
time for the initial design creation. But 
the result is more of a vision or a rough 
upfront design that guides subsequent 
work with the development team, not a 
fully developed blueprint that requires 
strict enforcement.
The architect then re˜nes the ini
-tial vision in direct cooperation with 
the development team and stakehold
-ers. His presence indicates that he takes 
their interests, concerns, and feedback 
seriously; his cooperation encourages 
and empowers them to actively shape 
the system™s design. Stakeholders are 
no longer receivers of the architecture 
but contributors to the ˜nal result. De
-cision chains and feedback loops con
-cerning the architecture move closer 
together in both space and time. The 
architectural vision is gradually stiff
-ened toward a walking skeleton and 
beyond.

 The process isn™t necessar
-ily straight; it often requires endurance 
over multiple iterations. But it™s worth 
itŠif you succeed, you™ll end up with 
a sustainable architecture that receives 
buy-in from all parties.
The lesson we can extract from 
this re˚ection is direct: t
he only 
feasible way for architects to

-ership. They must have a clear archi
-tectural vision for the system under de
-velopment. During its realization, they 
must keep the focus on the success-crit
-ical aspectsŠnot on all aspects at once 
but on one or two at a time. Each aspect 
should remain under focus long enough 
to achieve impact but short enough to 
not lose control over other aspects that 
require attention. Architects must con
-tinuously work toward measurable and 
realistic goalsŠshort and long termŠ

Leadership
