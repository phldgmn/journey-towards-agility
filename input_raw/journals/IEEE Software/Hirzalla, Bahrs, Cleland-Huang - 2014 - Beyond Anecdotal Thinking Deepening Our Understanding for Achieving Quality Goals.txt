Not to be confused with agile prac-

Achieving Qualities
Stakeholders™ quality concerns come 

in many different shapes and sizes, 
and it™s an important aspect of the 
requirements process to understand 

whether such concerns are achiev
-able or not. Business agility is just 
one example of a quality concern 
that, when relevant, must be care
-fully engineered into the delivered 

solution. However, there are many 
others. For example, to build a se
-cure system, we might call in secu
-rity experts who weigh up many dif
-ferent factors to ˜nd the right cost/
bene˜t balance for protecting assets. 

The same is true for a broad swathe 

of other quality concerns, such as re
-liability, performance, maintainabil
-ity, and scalability. 
While anecdotal evidence for 
achieving such quality concerns is 
clearly an important ingredient in 
bridging the gap between require
-ments and software development,  

our experiences showed that it™s also 
possibleŠand advisableŠto test and 
empirically validate the practices 

we adopt. As software engineers, 
we should lead the way in thinking 
about and challenging beliefs. In
-stead of jumping on various process 

bandwagons, as we™re sometimes apt 
to do, we can ask deeper questions 
about whether various solutions re
-ally deliver what they promise. In 

our case, we had initially taken for 
granted the ﬁfactﬂ that good gover
-nance would increase business agil
-ity, while in practice, the numbers 

didn™t end up supporting this conjec
-ture. Of course, this doesn™t mean we 
have to throw out SOA governance, 

but it does mean that we should un
-derstand its potentially retarding ef
-fect on achieving business agility.
Software requirements and 

development practices come 
and go. At the time we con
-ducted this study, everyone was talk
-ing about SOA and business agility. 
Today, conversations typically focus 
around big data, social computing, 
and predictive analytics. However, 

our underlying message remains the 

same. Whatever the current trend, 
we challenge you to think about 
practices that you might take for 

granted in your development pro
-cess. Put on your engineering hat, 

ask deeper questions, and take the 

time to empirically question your 

software engineering beliefs.
