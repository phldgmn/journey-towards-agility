Management in the Development 
of the Academic Agile Software 
Discourse: A Bibliometric AnalysisThomas G. Lechler

INTRODUCTIONSince the publication of the 
Agile Manifesto
 (Agile Alliance, 2001) 
agile models have taken a central role in the practice of software 
development (West, Grant, Gerush, & D™Silva, 2010; Dingsøyr, Nerur, 
Balijepally, & Moe, 2012). Following the agile principles, many 
different agile-based process models have been developed to improve 
individual and/or organizational software development productivity 
(Dingsøyr, Dybå, & Moe, 2010).
Software development process models provide the conceptual scheme for 
managing the software development process by directing and coordinating 
software development activities and resources (Boehm, 1988). Project 
management and agile concepts share many conceptual similarities and, 
not surprisingly, agile software development (ASD) is already been men
-tioned in the general project management standards (Project Management 
Institute [PMI], 2013). Some authors even see specific agile software devel
-opment models, such as Scrum, as a synonym for project management 
(Schwaber & Beedle, 2001).
Research has focused mainly on exploring the implementation, effective
-ness, and efficiency of agile models (Lindvall et al., 2002; Li, Moe, & Dybå, 
2010). It is unclear, however, whether and how project management methods 
and concepts could complement current agile methods. To better understand 
the potential merits, it is important to analyze the role project management 
has played in the development of the academic agile software development 
discourse.
In this study we identify the main intellectual structures of the academic 
agile discourse over time and explore how project management research 
was integrated into this discourse. We use bibliometric methods to reveal 
prominent research streams as well as their proximities and chronological 
relevance for the agile and project management communities. This method 
provides quantitative measures for evaluating the impact of project man
-agement research on the development of agile software development, their 
interrelations, and how they have evolved over time. The method has been 
successfully used to explore specific and more general questions in differ
-ent management disciplines, such as innovation management (Teichert, 
2004), operations management (Pilkington & Meredith, 2009), and project 
management (Artto, Martinsuo, Gemünden, & Murtoaro, 2009; Artto & 
Wikström, 2005; Hanisch & Wald, 2012). Some authors conducted compre
-hensive analyses of the agile discourse to identify main authors and research 
The practical applications of agile methods 
and their impact on the productivity and 
efficiency of software development domi
-nate the agile literature. We analyzed 827 
academic articles with bibliometric tech
-niques to explore the role project manage
-ment research played in the development of 
the academic agile discourse. Bibliometric 
analyses over two time periods reveal that 
project managementŒrelated topics form a 
distinct stream of research in the second 
time period but not in the first. Furthermore, 
our results reveal that the academic agile 
discussion has been mainly unidirectional. 
This situation offers many opportunities for 
project management researchers to contrib
-ute to the agile discourse.

The Foundation of Agile 
Software Development
The management of software develop
-ment projects is dominated by the rules 

and principles of the 
Agile Manifesto
 (Dingsøyr et al., 2012). The idea for the 

Agile Manifesto
 evolved from the critique 

of the inflexibility of traditional software 

development process models and their 

negative effects on the outcomes of soft
-ware development projects. The 
Agile 

Manifesto
 does not specify a specific 
software development model, but rather 

defines a platform of rules and prin
-
ciples that allow the derivation of many 

models with different attributes. Over 

the past decades, many agile-based soft
-ware development models have been 

introduced, including eXtreme Pro
-
gramming (XP), Scrum, lean software 

development, feature-driven develop
-
ment, and crystal methodologies, etc. 

(Dingsøyr et al., 2012).
Many researchers believe that agile-
based process models overcome the 

often criticized shortcomings by pro
-
viding greater flexibility, which leads 

to higher productivity (Cardozo, Neto, 

Barza, Franca, & da Silva, 2010) and 

efficiency (Li et al., 2010; Lindvall et al., 

2002). Because of these effects, agile-

based process models have quickly 

become the norm for software devel
-
opment. Unlike traditional linear planŒ

driven models, agile models are rooted in 

iterative and incremental steps by which 

software is developed through collab
-
oration between self-managed, cross-

functional teams (Conboy & Fitzgerald, 

2004). Flexibility is a fundamental 

characteristic of the process models as 

expressed by the 
Agile Manifesto
:ﬁIndividuals and interactions over 
processes and tools. Working software over 

comprehensive documentation. Customer 

collaboration over contract negotiation. 

Responding to change over following a 

plan
ﬂ (Agile Alliance, 2001, p. 1).
The resulting flexible, lightweight, 
and iterative processes facilitate 

changes and the ability to address a 

relatively unpredictable environment. 

As agile software development process 

models are becoming more and more 

popular among practitioners, academia 

is also showing increasing interest in 

this topic (Dingsøyr et al., 2012).
Methodology and Data
We used bibliometric coŒcitation analy
-
sis to quantitatively ascertain the struc
-tural patterns and research streams of 

the academic agile discourse (White 

& McCain, 1998; White, 1990; White 

& Griffith, 1981). It is based on the 
 underlying assumption that closely 
related works are frequently cited 

together (Culnan, 1986; Hoffman & Hol
-
brook, 1993; Tahai & Meyer, 1999) and 

it is used to identify the most influ
-
ential publications, authors, journals, 

and keywords, and to analyze relational 

links between them.
In this study, we employed the 
often used Document Co-citation 

Analysis (DCA) rather than Author Co-

citation Analysis (ACA). Author coŒ

citation analysis does not allow for the 

identification of central topics and their 

relationships (Teichert & Shehu, 2010), 

because many authors have contrib
-uted different thoughts and ideas to the 

agile discourse but they have also made 

contributions to other research fields. 

Using documents as the unit of analysis 

provides a more specific theme-related 

focus and precision about a research 

field other than the authorŒlevel analysis 

(Pilkington & Meredith, 2009; Teichert & 

Shehu, 2010).
The downside of using the docu
-ment co-citation analysis method is that 

a great amount of effort is necessary to 

identify accurate articles. Because of the 

different journal and conference cita
-
tion policies, or even misspellings, the 

database shows several entries for the 

same publication. All articles of the final 

selection of this study were carefully 

checked for these irregularities.
The basic procedure of extract
-ing common interests and capturing 

main research streams within the body 

of citations is the principle compo
-
nent analysis (PCA). In this study, we 

applied principle component analysis 

with Varimax rotation to achieve sim
-
plified expressions of extracted fac
-tors. Multi-dimensional scaling (MDS) 

is commonly used to obtain graphi
-
cal outputs (Ramos-Rodríguez & Ruíz-

Navarro, 2004; Nerur, Rasheed, & 

Natarajan, 2008); however, it is only 

possible to graphically present a multi-

dimensional scaling with two dimen
-
sions, which might not best fit the data 

(Culnan, 1986; Leydesdorff & Vaughan, 

2006; Pilkington & Meredith, 2009).

We applied UCINET (Borgatti, Everett,

In our analysis we covered the 
timeframe from 2001 through 2014. 
We chose the year 2001 as our starting 
point because the 
Agile Manifesto
 was 
published in that year. To reveal changes 
in the research streams of the agile dis
-course we split the 14-year timeframe 
into two time periods of seven years 
each (2001Œ2007 and 2008Œ2014). We 
did not choose shorter sub-periods 
because of the time delay between the 
initial publication and its citations. 
The seven-year range is sufficient to 
reveal basic structures in the discourse 
within the period, while also allow
-ing for the observation of changes 
across two periods (Nerur, Mahapatra, 
& Mangalaraj, 2008). The steps we took 
for our study are described in Figure 1.
We chose the Social Science Citation 
Index (SSCI) as the database for iden
-tifying relevant publications because it 
contains a very broad set of scientific 
journals, books, conference proceedings, 
and other scientific publications.
Identify Publications from SSCI 
database and Bibliometric Descriptives 
(Step 1)
A search for keywords in the Social Sci
-ence Citation Index database was used 
in order to identify agile publications. 
The keywords are names of specific agile 
models, agile project management, and 
their acronyms. We used project manage
-ment as an explicit keyword to identify 
those publications that discuss project 
managementŒrelated topics in the con
-text of agile and vice versa. Figure 2 illus
-trates the keywords and search logic.
1ﬁAgileﬂ is not a specified term that 
only appears in the software devel
-opment domain; it is also an impor
-tant term in the astroparticle physics, 
production, supply chain, and some 
other fields. Therefore, we manually 
excluded those articles that were not 
related to software development or 
the project management domain. We 
also limited the research area to four 
categories: ﬁcomputer science,ﬂ ﬁbusi
-ness economics,ﬂ ﬁoperational research 
management science,ﬂ and ﬁinformation 
science.ﬂ We only considered academic 
articles from journals and conferences 
because they better represent the 
academic discourse. Other authors, 
including Chuang et al. (2014) have 
identified the most influential authors 
and articles but their database included 
both academic and practitioner publica
-tions. Practitioner publications typically 
contain very few references and some 
do not have any reference lists and focus 
mainly on practical but not theoretical 
issues. Some journals and conference 
proceedings publish both scientific and 
practitioner articles, such as Commu
-nications of the ACM, 
Agile Conference
; therefore, we paid special attention to 
articles from these types of sources.

Citation Frequency Count (Step 2)
The data set we obtained from the pre
-vious step was split into two seven-year 
periods. The first seven-year period 
(2001Œ2007) contains 293 articles. The 
second seven-year period (2008Œ2014) 
contains 534 articles. A bibliometric 
software tool called BibExcel (Persson, 
Danell, & Schneider, 2009a, 2009b) 
was employed to compute citation and 
co-citation data. Since BibExcel only 
recognizes exactly coinciding terms, a 
manual normalization process is needed 
to guarantee accuracy. After processing 
data by BibExcel, the 293 identified 
articles from the first seven-year period 
resulted in approximately 1,390 refer
-ences. The 534 identified articles from 
the second seven-year period resulted 
in approximately 3,564 references. Then 
we constructed the citation frequency 
list, which is a rank-ordered list showing 
the frequency that each document was 
cited by each sub-period data set.
Co-citation Matrix Compilation (Step 3)
In order to decide which documents 
to include in the document co-citation 
analysis, two complimentary indicators 
were used: the Citations Received (CR) 
from each sub-period in our data
-base, and the overall Times Cited (TC) 
as listed in the Social Science Cita
-tion Index database. First, the num
-ber of citations received indicates the 
relevance of a document for the agile 
discourse. Typically, the top 1% to 5% 
of publications from the citation fre
-quency list is selected to conduct the 
analysis (Teichert, 2004; Small, 2006). 
In our study, we selected the top 5%. 
Second, the number of times cited from 
the Social Science Citation Index data
-base indicates the relevance of the doc
-ument to topics reaching beyond the 
agile discourse; therefore, an index was 
calculated for each sub-period by divid
-ing citations received with times cited. 
Higher values indicate a stronger focus 
on agile, whereas lower values indicate 
a broader orientation of an article. We 
have not considered articles with an 
index number lower than 0.02 because 
they should have a very low impact on 
the agile discourse. In this step, there
-fore, we removed all articles with a low 
index from the co-citation analysis.

Factor Analyses and Network Analyses 
(Step 4)
First, we performed principle compo
-nent factor analysis by using the two co-
citation matrices as data input. Varimax 
rotation was used to achieve simplified 
expressions of subfields, which are 
helpful to better identify and interpret 
the extracted factors. Factors are often 
extracted based on the similarity of arti
-cles. The most popular articles are more 
likely to load on multiple factors and 
consequently do not necessarily drive 
the factors (Pilkington & Meredith, 
2009). Horn™s Parallel Procedure was 
employed to decide how many factors 
should be retained in our study (Horn, 
1965; Glorfeld, 1995; Mertler &Vannatta, 
2002). The underlying theory of Horn™s 
Parallel Procedure is that factors from 
real data with a valid factor structure 
should have Eigenvalues greater than 
the 95th percentile of the Eigenvalues 
derived from random generated data 
with the same number of variables. First 
we generated a matrix of random data 
with the same size and the same number 
of variables as our co-citation matrix; 
then we ran the principle component 
analysis with our datasets and random 
datasets. By comparing the Eigenvalues 
of our principle component analysis 
with the 95th percentile of the random 
data principle component analysis, 
we kept only factors with Eigenvalues 
greater than the random data.
Based on Horn™s Parallel Procedure, 
we retrieved five factors for the first 
seven-year period, which account for 
63% of the total variance, and nine 
factors in the second seven-year period, 
which also account for 63% of the total 
variance. Only loadings above 
˚/˛0.45 
are reported, and only loadings greater 
than 
˚/˛0.7 are considered sufficient 
to interpret a certain factor (Nerur et al., 
2008). Approximately 10% of the articles 
loaded on more than two factors.
Factor analytical methods seek solu
-tions of maximal independence between 
factors and cannot provide any insights 
about how identified research streams 
are interrelated (Teichert, 2004). There
-fore, we used the network plot function 
of UCINET to generate a map based on 
the publications™ similarity in order to 
identify the interrelations between the 
research streams.

Network Plots
In this step we use a network map 
to visualize the identified factors of 
the document co-citation analysis and 
their relationships with each other. 
A close proximity of publications on 
the map usually suggests that there 
is considerable commonality between 
these publications. The first seven-year 
period network analysis presents a rela
-tively loose network structure, which 
is related to the adolescent status of 
the agile discourse within the observed 
time period and the relative low number 
of publications. The network plot is not 
presented here because the relations 
between the clusters are not offering 
new insights.
Figure 3 presents the network plot 
for the second seven-year period. For 
ease of presentation, the co-citation 
threshold was set to three, which means 
that only articles and their linkages are 
displayed when they are co-cited three 
times or more (Pilkington et al., 2009; 
Teichert et al., 2010). The plot shows 
that some topics are ﬁcloserﬂ in distance 
to each other than to others. Test-driven 
Development and Pair Programming, 
for example, are somewhat isolated self-
contained topics that are positioned 
on the periphery; both methods are 
used to improve software productivity 
on the individual developer level and 
the factors therefore represent discus
-sions of specific communities that are 
more isolated from the general agile dis
-course. This also applies to Factor 2-7 
representing the method engineering 
discussion as mentioned in sub-period 
one. The proximities of the other factors 
such as Agile Adoption (Factor 2-1), 
Agile Project Management (Factor 2-2), 
Agile and Plan-Driven (Factor 2-4), and 
Scrum (Factor 2-8) are more general 
topics and define the core of the aca
-demic agile discourse.
DiscussionExploring the Dynamic Development of 
the Academic Agile Discourse
We analyzed the academic agile dis
-course over two sub-periods to explore 
its time-dependent dynamics. A com
-parison of the identified intellectual 
structures between the first and the sec
-ond seven-year periods demonstrates: 
(1) that some topics from the previous 
period continue to be important and 
were even further developed; (2) that 
one topic faded (Empirical Analysis); 
and (3) that several new topics emerged 
(see Table 5).
In general, the discovered intel
-lectual structures for the two periods 
are consistent with other analyses of 
the agile discourse (Dingsøyr et al., 
2012); however, our time-based analy
-sis reveals some interesting dynamics 
in the intellectual structure. All agile-
specific topics such as agile adop
-tion, XP, test-driven development, and 
method engineering that we identified 
in the first period remain important for 
the academic discourse in the second 
period. Articles related to these top
-ics might not be the same between the 
two periods because some were highly 
cited in the first period but received low 
citations in the second period and vice 
versa. Only the topic of agile-related 
empirical studies ﬁdisappeared,ﬂ which 
is not surprising because this topic 
is broad and covers several aspects 
of agile. The differentiation of topics

(2008Œ2014) is characterized by a 
strong growth of agile-related academic 
research that is reflected in more than 
100 co-cited articles. The co-citation 
analysis revealed nine distinct fac
-tors. The main focus of the discourse 
remains on the introduction and imple
-mentation of new agile models (test-
driven development, XP, and Scrum). 
We also identified a distinct discourse of 
project managementŒrelated topics (see 
Factor 2-2). Of the nine factors, it is also 
the second strongest and takes a central 
role as it viewed in the network plot (see 
Figure 3). Other topics are more prac
-tice oriented, such as the use of agile 
methods in the global software develop
-ment context (Holmström et al., 2006).
The Role of Project 
Management in the 
Academic Agile Discourse
Project management research is becom
-ing increasingly relevant to the agile 
discourse. Despite the fact that several 
authors refer to the management of soft
-ware projects as agile project manage
-ment (Augustine et al., 2005; Sutherland 
et al., 2007; Fernandez & Fernandez, 
2008), it is surprising that we were not 
able to identify a distinct topic during the 
first time period. This clearly changed 
for the second period in which we iden
-tified 25 academic articles exploring 
the interaction between agile software 
development and project management 
concepts. These articles largely analyze 
the management challenges using agile 
methods (Sutherland et al., 2007; Korkala 
& Abrahamsson, 2007; Leybourne, 2009; 
Turk et al., 2005; Maruping et al., 2009; 
Dybå & Dingsøyr, 2008), and the inte
-gration of agile methods with existing 
traditional project management stan
-dards and processes (Abrahamsson et 
al., 2003; Coram & Bohner, 2005; McAvoy 
et al., 2009; Harris et al., 2009; Karlström 
et al., 2006). Another group of publica
-tions analyzes the conceptual differences 
between agile and planŒdriven methods 
(Abrahamsson et al., 2003; Boehm, 1988; 
Dybå et al., 2008; Sharp et al., 2008). 
Boehm and Turner (2004) discuss the 
problems involved in integrating agile 
and plan-driven methods and provide 
a ﬁhybrid modelﬂ to balance agility with 
existing software project management 
standards.
A specific analysis of agile project 
management publications reveals 
that the most frequently cited articles 
(Table 6) were primarily published in 
software and computer science jour
-nals; only one on the list was published 
in a management journal. All articles 
discuss consequences of agile for tra
-ditional software project management.
In sum, the agile discourse largely 
focuses on integrating agile principles 
into the management of software devel
-opment projects (Nerur, Mahapatra, & 
Mangalaraj, 2005; Leybourne, 2009). 
The reverse direction is rarely consid
-ered; in other words, project manage
-ment models have solely served as a 
subject of change by agile methods, 
yet project management research has 
not influenced the development and 
analysis of agile methods.

ConclusionsThe goals of this article were to discover 
distinct research streams in the agile 
movement, to address the changes in 
these streams over time, and clarify the 
role that project managementŒrelated 
themes played in the development of 
the academic agile discourse. We chose 
a bibliometric analysis to quantitatively 
analyze the academic agile literature. A 
thorough search in the Social Science 
Citation Index database identified 827 
agile-related publications. A limitation 
of this database is that it does not list all 
types of publications, for example, books 
and outlets other than journals and con
-ferences. This limitation did not directly 
affect our results because our research 
goal was to analyze the academic agile 
discourse; therefore, we focused on 
academic publications. Many practice-
oriented books and articles were cited 
in the identified academic publications; 
these practice-oriented publications are 
important for the general understand
-ing, use and dissemination of methods 
and models but they focus on practical 
problems and often do not address any 
specific research question or theoreti
-cal development. Another limitation is 
the relatively adolescent status of the 
agile discourse, which is reflected in the 
dynamics between the two time periods 
analyzed; it is very likely that some of the 
research streams that were identified 
might disappear or that others might 
evolve over the next few years. However, 
software developmentŒrelated research 
topics are aging very quickly, thus it 
seems that a 14-year time span should 
be sufficiently long to identify some 
fundamental intellectual structures in 
the discourse.
The motivation for our bibliometric 
analysis was to explore the impact of 
project management research on the 
agile software development discourse. 
For this analysis we deliberately chose 
a quantitative approach to gain a broad 
understanding of the state of the agile 
discourse and the role that project man
-agement takes. The majority of topics 
that we identified are related to specific 
agile methods, including test-driven 
development, eXtreme programming, 
pair programming, and Scrum. Further
-more, we could identify some concep
-tual discussions, such as the conceptual 
analysis of agile versus plan-driven 
approaches and method engineering.
Our results demonstrate that the 
management of projects is increasingly 
recognized in the agile software devel
-opment discourse, and that the man
-agement aspect has grown to become 
an important distinct topic. We have 
identified several research articles that 
compare or integrate agile project man
-agement with project management 
practices. This trend will continue, and 
agile principles are expected to play an 
important role in the future discourse 
of project management. Despite their 
affinity, it is not clear which role project 
management research has played in the 
development of the academic agile dis
-course; thus far, agile-related research 
is largely published within the outlets 
of the software development domain. 
In contrast, only few articles have been 
published in management research 
journals covering topics such as the 
adoption of agile in different contexts 
other than software development (Con
-forto, Salum, Amaral, da Silva, & de 
Almeida, 2014; Sommer, Hedegaard, 
DukovskaŒPopovska, & StegerŒJensen, 
2015). Several authors in the project 
management field are discussing how 
agile methods could be scaled to large 
projects (Petit & Besner, 2013; Dingsøyr, 
Fægri, & Itkonen, 2014; Hobbs & 
Petit, 2016).
However, the integration of agile 
topics into the project management dis
-course is mainly unidirectional. Classic 
topics in the project management dis
-course are currently underrepresented 
or have not yet found their way into the 
agile discourse. This situation provides 
an opportunity to analyze the central 
concepts of project management within 
the context of agile.
The management of risk and its 
different concepts and tools is a key 
process in the project management stan
-dards (Akintoye & MacLeod, 1997; Raz & 
Michael, 2001; Ward & Chapman, 2003), 
which is also relevant for agile software 
development but has yet to be picked up 
by the agile literature. Some other cen
-tral project management topics could 
be interesting for the further conceptual

  13development of agile: linking agile 
with the multi-project management 
and multi-tasking environment (Ebert 
& De Neve, 2001; De Maio, Verganti, 
& Corso, 1994); project governance 
(Abednego & Ogunlana, 2006), proj
-ect stakeholder management (Littau, 
Jujagiri, & Adlbrecht, 2010; Achterkamp 
& Vos, 2008); knowledge management 
and creation in the project environment 
(Bresnen, Edelman, Newell, Scarbrough, 
& Swan, 2003; Fong, 2003); linking strat
-egy to project and portfolio implementa
-tion (Meskendahl, 2010; De Reyck et al., 
2005), internal IT development versus 
external integration of contractors (Sun 
& Meng, 2009); and change control 
management (Kemerer, 1995; Wang, Ju, 
Jiang, & Klein, 2008).
Our study suggests the need for 
more conceptual research and the 
development of a theoretical perspec
-tive on agile. These areas of research 
will facilitate the conceptual integra
-tion of project managementŒrelated 
concepts into the agile discourse but 
might also influence the theoretical dis
-course in project management at large. 
Project management research offers 
interesting research questions for the 
agile discourse. This direction poses 
an attractive opportunity for project 
management researchers to publish 
agile-related research in management-
oriented research outlets.
