 2. Technical
 background
 Proponents
 of OOAD
 argue
 that objects are
 a natural
 way
 to building software
 systems
 because
 they
 reﬂect
 the real-world
 enti- ties of a domain. This facilitates
 organization
 of IT system
 elements
 and relating
 them to the real-world
 entities. However,
 business users and analysts
 generally
 think of a business system
 as a collec- tion of process
 elements
 arranged
 to support unique
 organizational
 processes.
 This includes business logic, rules, and constraints
 that govern
 the process.
 In many
 cases, a process
 element supporting a single discrete
 business function needs
 to span multiple objects. For
 example,
 a process
 element representing
 discrete
 business functionality,
 such
 as “verify
 order,”needs
 to use multiple object classes like
 Customer,
 Order,
 and Item,
 and needs
 to include business logic, rules, and constraints
 to verify
 the order.
 An “order
 veriﬁcation”businesscomponentcanencapsulatealloftheabove
 functionality required
 to support the “verify
 order”process
 ele- ment. Hence, business components
 address
 one of the criticisms of OOAD,
 the relatively
 low
 level
 of abstraction
 of objects and the lack
 of consistent
 connection to business-oriented
 concepts—speciﬁcally
 to the elements
 of business processes—thereby
 adversely
 affecting
 application development
 time and complexity
 ( Brown
 20 0 0 ). The proponents
 of CBD argue
 that it reduces
 the skill re-
 quirements
 of analysts.
 In other
 words,
 it is not
 necessary
 for a developer
 to acquire
 signiﬁcant experience
 in systems
 analysis
 and design before
 he or she has to model a business system
 through
 component
 reuse
 ( Sprott
 20 0 0 ). On the other
 hand, prior research
 has shown
 that experience
 plays
 a signiﬁcant role
 in modeling systems
 using OOAD
 ( Agarwal
 et al. 1996b;
 Khatri et al. 2006
 ). However,
 the role
 of experience
 has not
 been
 examined
 in the context
 of reuse,
 which
 is the focus of this study.

 while coupling refers

 The ideal scenario is one in which

 is maximized

 components
 is minimized,
 and objectives

 maintainability,
 and development
 cost
 are
 also optimized.
 Vitharana
 et al. (2004)
 pro-
 posed
 a strategy-based
 approach
 for designing reusable
 business components.
 This approach
 was
 further enhanced
 for service design and implemented
 in a CASE
 tool
 ( Jain et al. 2007
 ). Erl
 et al. (2009)
 proposed
 a web
 service contract
 design and versioning
 methodology.
 Birkmeier
 and Overhage
 (2009)
 provide
 an excellent
 review
 of component
 identiﬁcation and design approaches.
 In this study,
 the subjects used
 the UML notation
 for develop-
 ing the conceptual
 object and component
 models. Note,
 however,
 that other
 tools
 such
 as ArchiMate
 could also be used
 as an alternative
 for developing

 models ( Jonkers
 et al. 2011
 ). The method
 implemented
 by Jain et al. 2007
 in a CASE
 tool
 was
 used
 to design the components/services.
 The approach
 takes
 an analysis
 level
 object model, representing
 a business domain, as the input
 and generates
 potential
 component/service
 designs, in which
 the classes in the object model are
 grouped
 into
 appropriate
 components/services
 based
 on high cohesion and low
 coupling between
 the classes. Next,
 employing
 a multiobjective
 genetic
 algorithm,
 it optimizes
 a set
 of managerial
 objectives,
 such
 as ease of assembly,
 maintainability,
 and cost
 effectiveness,
 to identify a set
 of non-dominated
 solutions. Finally,
 a preferred
 component
 design solution is selected
 from
 this set.
 The components
 designed
 for the insurance
 claim domain using the above
 approach
 were

 for reuse
 by a large
 global IT ﬁrm. The creation
 of services does require

 objects, which

 An IDE, like

 by Oracle
 or provided
 in IBM WebSphere
 ( IBM 2016
 ), was

 The availability

 made the process

 components/services
 from
 constituent
 objects quite

 model The focus of this study
 is on comparing

 components
 with that of analysts
 using a library
 of reusable
 objects. Our study
 can therefore

 and Kang 2005

 corresponds

 in CBD, a class corresponds

 in OOAD.

 claims domain. Each

 and focus, prior experience

 and design could have
 a bearing
 on conceptual
 modeling performance. Agarwal
 et al. (1996b)
 conducted
 a study

 et al. 2003a

 for the object-oriented
 and component-based

 in the class and component

 The productions
 or inference

 on the data structures

 part of the knowledge

 and CBD principles and techniques
 that the modeler stores

 An attention
 management
 system
 determines
 which
 part of the data structure
 the modeler is focusing on, so that the program
 operators
 can be triggered
 ( Larkin
 and Simon 1987
 ). Selecting the right objects or components
 entails

 for assets
 that can be reused
 in a given
 situation, based

 of the speciﬁc representation.
 Attention
 management
 is necessary
 for this search

 Larkin
 and Simon (1987)
 contend
 that a major
 source
 of computational
 inequivalence
 between
 two
 representations
 is the difference
 in the search
 strategies
 employed.
 A component
 is a representation

 business function, and each

 interfaces
 provides
 a speciﬁc service. It facilitates
 effective
 attention
 management

 cohesive
 components

 operations

 a service. In contrast,
 the focus of the object represen-
 tation
 is on problem
 domain entities and concepts;
 the operations

 a speciﬁc service are
 typically
 distributed
 across
 several
 objects, thereby
 rendering
 attention
 management
 much
 less effective.
 Attending
 to and processing
 fewer
 information cues reduces
 the task
 complexity
 ( Wood
 1986
 ) of component-based
 modeling vis-à-vis object-oriented
 modeling. Our research
 model (see Fig.
 4 ) depicts the moderating
 inﬂuence of prior analysis
 and design experience
 on modeling per-
 formance. Several
 prior studies
 (e.g., Agarwal
 et al. 1996b;
 Khatri et al. 2006;
 Vessey
 and Galletta
 1991
 ) have
 examined
 the role
 of experience,
 IS domain knowledge,
 and skills. Based
 on experience,
 experts
 are
 able to develop
 hierarchically-organized
 schemata
 ( Chi et al. 1982
 ). They
 are
 able to activate
 higher-level
 schemata
 that are
 relevant
 ( LaFrance
 1989

 research
 has shown
 that experts
 are
 good
 at automating

 details
 novices

 attentive

 focusing instead
 on larger-sized
 functional chunks

 1986
 ). The systems

 decomposition

 . Decomposition

 The objects available

 sulting from

 process

 analysis
 task.
 We would
 like
 to point out that prior studies
 investigating
 modeling effectiveness
 have
 dealt with models generated
 from
 scratch.
 In those studies,
 developing

 class diagram,
 for instance,
 entailed
 creating
 the classes, attributes,
 and opera-
 tions, and then connecting the classes through
 relationships.
 In contrast,
 we are
 investigating
 conceptual
 model development
 from
 a reuse
 standpoint,
 where
 the needed

 already
 been
 designed
 and stored
 in a library.
 Among the advantages
 claimed
 by CBD proponents
 is the one that a system

 ments ( Due 20 0 0; Sprott
 20 0 0 ). As Sprott
 (20 0 0) notes,
 “The parts

 software

 different
 manufacturers

 The assemblers do not

A component
 represents

 et al. 1996a;

 well-deﬁned

 cohesion of a component

This makes

 5. Hypotheses
 After
 the assets
 are
 selected
 for reuse,
 they
 are
 assem- bled
 together
 to form the model of the business system
 (see Fig.
 3 ). For
 the assembly
 process
 to take
 place, the selected
 ob- jects/components
 need
 to be ﬁrst
 included
 in a class/component
 diagram.
 In a component
 diagram,
 a component
 provides
 explicit
 cues for dependency connections from
 other
 components
 by ex-
 plicitly
 exposing
 its
 services through
 its
 interfaces.
 In contrast,
 an object in a class diagram
 does not
 provide
 such
 explicit
 cues; con- nections to other
 objects are
 implicit
 in the structural
 relationships
 that exist
 in the problem
 domain. Hence, we can claim that the connections provided
 in the component
 data structure
 are
 more
 effective
 for asset
 assembly
 than the object data structure.
 As Larkin
 and Simon (1987
 , pp. 67–68)
 state,
 “the nature
 of attention
 management
 depends crucially
 on the linkages
 provided
 in the data structure.”
We therefore
 ﬁnd that, from
 the standpoint
 of granularity,
 decomposition
 quality,
 and focus, a component
 is computationally
  modular,
 and service-focused.
 The information empha-
 sized
 in both
 the component
 representation
 and the systems
 anal- ysis
 task
 is service-oriented,
 resulting
 in cognitive
 ﬁt ( Vessey
 1991
 ). Cognitive
 ﬁt allows
 the analyst
 to develop
 a consistent
 mental rep-
 resentation
 to operate
 on and subsequently
 exhibit
 superior con- ceptual
 modeling performance. Also, because
 of the right granular-
 ity and high decomposition
 quality,
 the component
 data structure
 enables better
 attention
 management
 and lower
 complexity
 during the search
 process.
 We therefore
 posit the following
 hypothesis:
 H1 : Systems
 analysts
 develop
 more
 accurate
 conceptual
 mod- els of business systems
 by reusing
 components
 than by reusing
 objects. Experience with decomposition-recomposition
 activities
 helps an experienced
 analyst
 learn to perform the low-level
 details
 automatically
 and concentrate
 on larger
 chunks
 of the problem.
 So while we can expect
 an inexperienced
 analyst
 to focus on the “low-level”objects—missing
 the forest
 for the trees—an
 ex-
 perienced
 analyst
 is able to mentally
 activate
 the higher-level
 schemata
 that she possesses to recognize
 large
 chunks
 of objects at a higher level
 of abstraction.
 Much
 like
 a chess
 master
 who is able to recognize
 larger
 sized
 chunks
 on a chess
 board
 and take
 appropriate
 actions to deal with the situation ( Chase and Simon 1973
 ), an experienced
 analyst
 can exploit
 hierarchically-organized
 knowledge
 structures
 to form a mental representation
 consisting
 of cohesive
 chunks—which
 hide the low-level
 operations
 of and coupling among objects—and generate
 a superior conceptual
 model. We therefore
 posit the following
 hypothesis:
 H2 : Analysts
 with higher experience
 in systems
 analysis
 and design techniques
 develop
 more
 accurate
 conceptual
 models of business systems
 when reusing
 objects. As discussed
 before,
 because
 of their decomposi-
 tion/recomposition
 skills, experienced
 analysts
 can identify relevant
 objects and then assemble them to arrive
 at a solution more
 effectively
 than less experienced
 analysts.
 That is something
 that inexperienced
 analysts,
 who do not
 possess such
 skills, are
 not
 adept
 at. The picture
 is markedly
 different
 for CBD. Inex-
 perienced
 analysts
 do not
 have
 to deal with lower-level
 objects anymore.
 Instead,
 what they
 ﬁnd in the repository
 is components
 that encapsulate
 service-oriented
 composites
 at the interfaces.
 The hierarchically-organized
 decomposition/recomposition
 schemata
 that provided
 experienced
 analysts
 with a signiﬁcant advantage
 in identifying chunks
 of objects and assembling them are
 no longer
 that necessary
 because
 components
 replicate
 those mental chunks
 physically
 in external
 memory
 (repository).
 So the handicap that inexperienced
 analysts
 face
 with respect
 to decomposi-
 tion/recomposition
 knowledge
 structures
 is compensated
 for by the high-level
 chunking
 of reusable
 service-oriented
 components
 available
 in external
 memory—the
 repository.
 We therefore
 posit that:
 H3 : Analysts
 with higher experience
 in systems
 analysis
 and design techniques
 do not
 develop
 more
 accurate
 conceptual
 models of business systems
 when reusing
 components.

 6.1.

 Our research

 4 ) examines
 the inﬂuence of the object and component

 for examining

 is Performance
 (details

 of this variable

 The independent variable
 we wish to manipulate
 is Chunk Representation
 . We use this variable
 to deﬁne two
 groups

 ), one in which
 modelers develop

 analysis
 of variance

 (factor)

 two
 or more

 Any

 groups

 to validity
 In empirical
 software
 engineering research,
 it is important
 to minimize the threats
 to the validity
 of the research
 ﬁndings. In this section, we discuss how
 we addressed
 different
 types of validity
 threats,
 including those to construct
 validity,
 internal
 validity,
 and external
 validity.
 Construct
 validity
 is concerned
 with whether
 a test
 measures
 the intended
 construct.
 Threats
 to construct
 validity
 could arise from
 the choice
 of treatment
 and the choice
 of outcome measure.
 The choice
 of the treatment
 involves
 operationalization
 of the independent variable
 and the administration
 of the treatment,
 and the choice
 of outcome measure
 involves
 operationalization
 of the dependent variable
 and the administration
 of the measurement.
 We conducted
 a pilot
 study
 to test
 construct
 validity
 before
 running the actual experiment.
 Two
 subjects used
 OOAD
 and two
 subjects used
 CBD to complete
 the experimental
 tasks,
 after
 which
 they
 were
 debriefed.
 They
 were
 satisﬁed
 with the training
 and the experimental
 materials;
 based
 on the suggestion
 from
 one of the participants,
 we included
 a practice
 exercise
 that subjects in the actual experiment
 completed
 prior to participating in the actual experiment.
 In this study
 the antecedent
 (independent variable)
 is the representation
 provided
 by the method
 used,
 which
 is either object or component
 . Because
 the participants
 were
 divided
 into
 two
 groups,
 with one group
 asked
 to model a business system
 by reusing
 objects, and the other
 group
 asked
 to do the same by reusing
 components,
 there
 was
 no threat
 to construct
 validity
 with respect
 to the operationalization
 of the independent variable.
 Also, when we presented
 the treatments
 to the participants,
 they
 had no clue about the results
 we were
 expecting
 from
 the experiment;
 the administration
 of the treatments,
 therefore,
 did not
 pose any
 threat
 to construct
 validity.
 The outcome measure
 of interest
 is Performance
 , which
 indi- cates
 how
 well
 a subject modeled
 the business system
 by reusing
 objects or components.
 As discussed
 in detail
 above,
 we measured
 performance using two
 scores:
 objective
 (CM Obj ) and subjective
 (CM Sub ). The formula for the objective
 score
 was
 based
 on existing
 knowledge
 in conceptual
 modeling, while the subjective
 score,
 which
 reﬂects
 the overall
 quality
 of the developed
 model, was
 assigned
 independently
 by two
 experts.
 As reported
 before,
 the intraclass
 correlation
  between
 CM Obj and CM Sub was
 quite
 high, suggesting
 strongly
 the validity
 of our scoring schemes.
 Also, the inter-rater
 reliability
 between
 the two
 experts
 on the subjective
 score
 was
 very
 high, further strengthening
 the construct
 validity
 of our study.
 We tested
 our hypotheses
 using a well-designed,
 controlled
 experiment
 where
 any
 systematic
 bias—a source
 of threat
 to internal
 validity—was
 effectively
 addressed
 by randomly
 assigning subjects to the two
 groups.
 The desired
 effects
 of randomization
 were
 achieved;
 the two
 groups
 were
 similar with respect
 to the demographic/experience
 attributes.
 We also took
 precautions
 against
 any
 other
 plausible
 explanation
 for the study’s
 ﬁndings by including covariates
 (SA&D
 Experience, OOA&D
 Experience, Pro-
 gramming
 Experience, OO Programming
 Experience, and Insurance
 Knowledge),which
 factored
 out any
 differential
 effects
 they
 may
 have
 on the dependent variable.
 Threats
 to external
 validity
 can arise from
 non-representative
 sampling
 or a non-representative
 research
 context.
 One criticism that can be levelled
 against
 our research
 is that the grain
 size of the components
 was
 much
 larger
 than that of the classes used
 in the study.
 Because
 of that, the number
 of components
 was
 much
 less than the number
 of classes in the libraries
 that participants
 used.
 However,
 both
 libraries
 capture
 the same insurance
 claim domain completely,
 so they
 are
 informationally
 equivalent.
 Ad-
 ditionally,
 these libraries
 were
 built by an IT ﬁrm for use in the insurance
 domain, and can be considered
 to be representative
 of situations in the real
 world.
 From

 10.
 Conclusions and future
 directions
 In this study,
 we conducted
 an empirical
 investigation
 into
 the effectiveness
 of the object and component
 representations
 for modeling business systems.
 Our work
 should be viewed
 within the context
 of software
 reuse
 research
 because
 the participants
 in this study
 modeled
 systems

 objects/components

 an existing
 library.
 While several
 proponents
 have
 argued
 about the beneﬁts
 of CBD, our study
 addresses
 a major
 gap
 in the literature
 by demonstrating,
 using a well-controlled
 experiment,
 that component-based
 reuse
 is indeed
 more
 effective
 than object- oriented
 reuse
 for modeling business systems.
 All the participants
 were
 IT professionals
 working
 for a global IT consulting ﬁrm, so the usual criticisms leveled
 against
 the use of novices
 or student
 subjects do not
 apply.
 The evaluation
 of the two
 approaches
 was
 also very
 comprehensive
 in that the experimental
 tasks
 included
 modeling a system
 from
 the perspective
 of selecting the right assets
 and assembling those assets.
 Prior research
 in cognitive
 psy-
 chology
 and human factors
 provided

 The most

 ﬁnding of our study
 is the interaction
 between
 chunk
 representation
 and experience.
 As hypothesized
 in our research
 model, we found the presence
 of moderating
 effects
 of systems
 analysis
 and design experience
 on the relationship
 between
 systems
 development
 method
 and conceptual
 modeling performance. The service-oriented
 abstraction
 provided
 by com- ponents
 and their interfaces
 allows
 a less experienced
 analyst
 to perform on par with more
 experienced
 analysts.
 The participants
 in this study
 had very
 little or no knowl-
 edge
 of the automobile
 insurance
 domain. An interesting
 future
 direction
 would
 be to examine
 if knowledge
 of the application domain has any
 bearing
 on modeling performance. Shaft
 and Vessey
 (1995)
 conducted
 a study
 in the area

 hension and found that programmers

 with the domain use a hierarchical

 comprehension
 process,
 as opposed

 (2017)

 The interesting
