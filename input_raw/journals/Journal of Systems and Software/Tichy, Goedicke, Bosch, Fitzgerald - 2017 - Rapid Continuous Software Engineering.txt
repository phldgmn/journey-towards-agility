 Rapid
 Continuous Software
 Engineering The is the 2nd special issue focusing on the challenges
 and is- sues related
 to software
 engineering for processes
 supporting rapid
 continuous software
 engineering approaches.
 These are
 mainly
 ar-
 eas where
 the overall
 approach
 can be characterized
 as releasing
 quickly
 and as often
 as possible to address
 the users’ need
 in a much
 better
 way
 than the more
 classical style
 of “top
 down”devel-
 opment. There
 are,
 of course, areas
 of software
 development
 where
 components
 and systems
 needs
 certiﬁcation e.g. aerospace,
 train
 and automotive
 and the related
 regulations
 differ
 from
 country
 to country.
 But even
 in such
 cases, software
 systems
 contain parts
 and subsystems
 which
 can best
 be developed
 through
 including the user in a structured
 and systematic
 way
 during the develop-
 ment on a continuous basis. The call for this issue sought to solicit papers on recent
 research
 results
 which
 could help advance
 knowledge
 as to how
 traditional
 software
 engineering aspects
 can be translated
 into
 ways
 of work-
 ing in the rapid
 continuous development
 mode. In total
 14 submis- sions were
 received,
 and following
 a stringent
 review
 process,
 four papers were
 accepted
 for eventual
 publication. These papers are
 brieﬂy
 discussed
 below.
 All accepted
 contributions describe
 and evaluate
 approaches
 which
 empower
 developers
 and users in these agile scenarios as they
 sought to address
 the processes
 with re-
 spect to their speed
 and potential
 information overﬂow.
 Thus, the need
 to better
 understand
 and hence provide
 the ability to con- trol
 the development
 processes
 is addressed
 in the contributions contained
 this special issue: “Going with the ﬂow:
 An activity
 theory
 analysis
 of ﬂow
 tech-
 niques
 in software
 development”by
 Dennehy
 and Conboy
 presents
 an empirical
 study
 of ﬂow
 activities
 in two
 companies
 which
 are
 in the process
 of adopting
 ﬂow
 techniques.
 The empirical
 study
 is based
 on activity
 theory
 to study
 multiple, not
 singular,
 activities
 and investigates
 contradictions
 and congruencies when people in- teract
 and use artifacts.
 Hence, the authors
 investigate
 the moti-
 vation
 of the companies
 to adopt
 those techniques
 as well
 as ma- jor contradictions
 and congruencies in their implementation
 and use. They
 identiﬁed
 four key contradictions,
 including that ﬂow
 practices
 provide
 greater
 visibility into
 what teams
 are
 doing but can also lead to the perception
 by software
 engineering teams
 that management
 will use the tools
 to control
 developers.
 Roles
 in software
 engineering teams
 and projects
 differ
 be-
 tween
 classical software
 engineering processes
 and agile ones. The paper “Transition
 of organizational
 roles
 in Agile transformation
 process:
 A grounded
 theory
 approach”by
 Jovanovic
 et al. inves-
 tigates
 how
 those roles
 change
 when companies
 introduce
 agile software
 engineering processes.
 Based
 on interviews
 as well
 as observations,
 the authors
 identify multiple aspects
 of inadequate
 transformations
 to agile roles,
 e.g., causes,
 conditions, contingen-
 cies, consequences.
 While the focus is on roles,
 the paper also presents
 general
 challenges
 and solutions with respect
 to agile transformation.
 Continuous experimentation
 aims to reduce
 development
 risks
 by iteratively
 testing
 assumptions
 that are
 critical to the success of the software.
 In their paper “Introducing
 continuous experimenta-
 tion in large
 software-intensive
 product
 and service organisations”,
 Yaman
 et al. investigate
 how
 continuous experimentation
 is intro-
 duced
 in development
 processes
 using a multiple case study
 on two
 Finnish
 companies.
 Identiﬁed
 beneﬁts
 include better
 customer
 insight and reduced
 development
 effort.
 Challenges
 are,
 for exam-
 ple, the lack
 of experimentation
 skills and knowledge,
 as well
 as  to access end users. User acceptance
 testing
 is an important
 activity
 in classical and agile software
 engineering projects.
 In their paper “User ac- ceptance
 testing
 for Agile-developed
 web-based
 applications: Em- powering
 customers
 through
 wikis and mind maps”, Otaduy and Diaz present
 an approach
 using wikis and mind maps to empower
 customers
 to perform user acceptance
 testing
 on their own.
 Wiki pages
 are
 created
 by the developers
 to support acceptance
 testing
 by customers
 and include starting
 pages
 for tests
 as well
 as test-
 ing hints.
 Additionally,
 they
 store
 executable
 acceptance
 test
 scripts
 deﬁned
 by the user.
 As users lack
 the expertise
 to write
 these test
 scripts,
 the approach
 contains a mind-map-based
 graphical
 domain speciﬁc language
 for the modeling of acceptance
 tests.
 Last
 but not
 least,
 we would
 also like
 to thank the authors
 of all submissions and the reviewers
 for their dedication
 and commit- ment in providing

 review
 process
 enabling this publi- cation. Without their effort
 s, the software
 engineering community would
 not

 this important
 opportunity to study
 the disruptive
 changes
 that arise through
 the adoption
 of rapid
 continuous soft-
 ware
 engineering development
 processes.
