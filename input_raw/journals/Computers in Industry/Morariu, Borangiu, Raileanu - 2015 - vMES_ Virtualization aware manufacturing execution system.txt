IntroductionThe trend towards virtualization of software systems gainsmore and more traction in modern enterprises in this decade.There are a series of immediate beneﬁts offered by virtualizationfor almost any kind of software system; better resource utilization,improved ﬂexibility and higher availability, which translatedirectly in reduced operational costs for the enterprise. Virtualiza-tion also represents the main enabling technology for cloudcomputing, regardless of the delivery method considered. Todayenterprises can take advantage of public cloud offerings toprovision virtual workloads (IaaS), applications on speciﬁc plat-forms (PaaS) or access in multi-tenant applications (SaaS) in a pay-per-use model, with no initial investment required. At the sametime, complex software solutions can be based on variouscombinations of cloud domains and delivery methods. Theseadvances in how technology can be provisioned and consumedacross the Internet in real time open the door to the developmentof new business models and give a new degree of agility to the ITdepartments supporting core business functions of any enterprise.From a location domain perspective we can consider fourestablished cloud delivery models: public clouds, communityclouds, private clouds and hybrid clouds. Public clouds are designedto be used by a variety of different customers (users or enterprises)and are usually built around open standards. From a costperspective the services can be either free or in a pay per usagemodel. Community clouds are shared by several organizations andsupport a speciﬁc community that has shared concerns (e.g.,mission, security requirements, policy, and compliance consider-ations). It may be managed by the organizations or a third partyand may exist on premise or off premise. Private clouds are builtinside enterprise boundaries and are designed to offer services tobusiness units of that enterprise only. Private clouds are costeffective only for large enterprises that can assure an optimal usageof cloud resources. For small enterprises a private cloud would bean overhead from a cost perspective, as they will still have to ownand maintain the hardware and software infrastructure.
 Hybridclouds are a combination of the above mentioned two models,where both public and private services are composed in order toprovide enhanced functionality. Manufacturing enterprises have started to adopt cloud com-puting, especially in the upper side of the software stack;enterprise resource planning (ERP), supply chain management(SCM) and customer resource management (CRM) areas. One of theestablished examples in these regards is Salesforce.com [1] whichoffers a complete range of business supporting applications in aSaaS delivery model. However, cloud adoption in industry is drivenmainly by cost reduction and so it is opportunistic in nature. Thereis no systematic approach to cloud adoption that can align themanufacturing industry organizational standards like ISA-95 tothe above mentioned cloud delivery models, in a consistentfashion. ISA-95 [2] deﬁnes 5 levels for the hierarchical organizationof manufacturing enterprises, as follows: Levels 0, 1 and 2 represent the process control levels and theirobjective is to control the physical shop ﬂoor equipment in orderto execute the actual production operations that result in one ormore ﬁnished products; Level 3 is the MES (Manufacturing Execution System) level andconsists of several activities that have to be executed in order toprepare, monitor and complete the production process executedat level 0, 1 and 2. These activities are scheduling, qualitymanagement, maintenance, production tracking, and othersdepending on the speciﬁcs of each enterprise. Several formalspeciﬁcations for MES have been attempted [3]. Private clouds, inIaaS delivery method are suited for this ISA level. Level 4 is the ERP (Enterprise Resource Planning) level, whereﬁnancial and logistic activities are executed. These activities arenot integrated in real time to the production and include: longterm planning, marketing and sales, procurement, and so on.Public clouds, in SaaS delivery method are suited for this ISA level.The term cloud computing is widely used to describe anyinformation system that is delivering computing services over theInternet [4]. From this perspective, cloud computing represents afairly new model for delivering IT services to consumers (end users,enterprises, organizations, etc.) by providing computation power,software, data access, networking and storage in an on demandfashion. The main novelty in this delivery model is that cloudproviders are offering computing services in the form of a utility,similar to the concept used to deliver electricity to provide energyto end users in the past. The concept of cloud computing was ﬁrstintroduced back in 1960s by John McCarthy [5]; however, due totechnological limitations of that time, his vision never got enoughtraction in the industry to materialize. Since then, there wereseveral technological breakthroughs in the virtualization area thatallowed the emergence of cloud computing.A common used deﬁnition of cloud computing is the oneprovided by National Institute of Standards and Technology [6],which deﬁnes the main cloud characteristics, as follows: On-demand self-service. A consumer can unilaterally provisioncomputing capabilities, such as server time and network storage,as needed automatically without requiring human interactionwith each service’s provider. Broad network access. Capabilities are available over the network,being accessed through standard mechanisms that promoteusage by heterogeneous thin or thick client platforms (e.g.,mobile phones, laptops, and PDAs). Resource pooling. The provider’s computing resources are pooledto serve multiple consumers using a multi-tenant model, withdifferent physical and virtual resources dynamically assignedand reassigned according to consumer demand. There is a senseof location independence
 in that the customer generally has nocontrol or knowledge over the exact location of the providedresources but may be able to specify a location at a higher level ofabstraction (e.g., country, state, or datacentre). Examples ofresources include storage, processing, memory, network band-width, and virtual machines. Rapid elasticity. Capabilities can be rapidly and elasticallyprovisioned, in some cases automatically, to quickly scale outand rapidly released to quickly scale in. To the consumer, thecapabilities available for provisioning often appear to beunlimited and can be purchased in any quantity at any time. Measured

Additionally, other cloud characteristics can be considered, asfollows: Multi-tenancy refers to the fact that the same resource instance isbeing used concurrently by multiple cloud customers (end usersor organizations). This can be seen at multiple layers, because ineach model there is some resource being shared: the actualhardware box, the hypervisor, the platform or the actualsoftware application. This approach brings a huge advantagein terms of resource usage optimization, but raises a number ofchallenges, specially related to customer data security. Pay-per-use model refers to the charge back model adopted bycloud providers. This allows a low start-up investment fromcustomers and at the same time allowing them to use high endtechnologies.The most important adoption driver for cloud computing is theconversion of traditional computing systems to virtualizedenvironments. The virtualization of a computing system in generalmeans that hardware and software resources are organized andmanaged in a pool model, allowing multiple beneﬁciaries to usethese resources as needed. This approach leads directly to a higherresource utilization, which represents the primary goal and beneﬁtfor any form of cloud computing. Historically, computer systemslike databases, Business Intelligence (BI) servers and so on, weredeployed on dedicated physical infrastructures,
 or in other words,dedicated servers and storage usually interconnected
 withdedicated network as well. Some of the most common reasonsfor this practice were the poor standard adherence of legacyimplementation, very narrow certiﬁcation margins imposed bysoftware vendors, no real option to impose hard resourceutilization quotas at operating system level, and many others.The result of this practice was that the overall resource utilizationwas below 20% on average [7]. The pressure to reduce costs byoptimization of resource utilization has set the scene for afundamental conceptual change towards virtualization as longterm strategy inside large enterprises.Virtualization technologies available today allow creatingvirtual instances of various physical devices of a computingenvironment like CPUs, memory, storage, networking, USB portsand various other devices and ports as required. The virtualizationof these components is achieved by introducing an intermediatesoftware layer between the physical resources and the operatingsystem layer called hypervisor [8–10]. A hypervisor is transparentfor the guest operating system and manages the virtual resourcesand their mapping to the physical resource layer. In the last decade,hypervisors have evolved and provide a rich set of featuresallowing not only ﬁne grained management of physical resourcesbut also many real time optimizations. Technically, private clouds can be built from existingcomputing resources by using a cloud management software stackor can be purchased, as turn-key solutions, from established cloudvendors like IBM, Oracle, Microsoft, RedHat and others. Manufacturingenterprises have to adopt these new technologies in order toadvance to a new level of mass customization where ﬂexibility,scalability and agility are the differentiating factors for real-timecontrol manufacturing

The end goal would be to enable acustomer-driven resource allocation at MES level through real timeanalysis of the computing capacity required to manufacture theproducts associated with a given product batch.This paper aims at providing a structured approach to cloudadoption and MES virtualization for manufacturing companies thatare organized using the ISA-95 models, focusing especially on level3, where private clouds can offer support for a virtualized MES. The research challenge here consists in thedesign of a system capable to drive the vMES solution, adapted tothe ISA-95 workload model. At the heart ofthe service oriented manufacturing system is the enterpriseservice bus/manufacturing service bus coupling. The EnterpriseService Bus (ESB) was ﬁrst proposed by [31] as a softwarearchitecture having a set of key characteristics:
 Message routing and control across enterprise components;
 Decoupling of various modules by asynchronous messaging,replacing point to point communication with the common busarchitecture; Promote reusability of utility services, reducing the number ofredundant services across the enterprise; Provide transformation and translation of messages to allow easyintegration of legacy applications; Provide an engine for workﬂow execution.As present, all major software companies provide solidcommercial implementation of ESB on top of their SOA offerings:IBM WebSphere ESB, Microsoft BizTalk Server or Oracle EnterpriseService Bus. The vMES process ﬁlls the gapbetween the virtualized workloads and the underlying privatecloud architecture, allowing intelligent and efﬁcient usage of cloudresources for MES execution. The BPEL process proposed isillustrated in Fig. 3.The BPEL process is invoked by the Customer Order Manage-ment module (situated at Level 4 in the enterprise hierarchy) [33],when a customer order needs to be executed. The customer orderencapsulates the production schedule of the entire product batchordered by the customer, together with the time constraintsassociated. The BPEL process itself is exposed as a web service endpoint (vMES_EndPoint) and can be invoked through the upper levelESB implementation in a standard fashion. The process is designedin two sections (Workloads_Management
 and Event_Manage-ment) that execute in parallel.Section I: Workloads_Management
 starts with a Java embeddingactivity that provides an algorithm that computes the initialprovisioning plan, based on the product batch. Speciﬁcally theproduct types and the batch size are the two components thatwould decide what workloads would be provisioned at MESlevel and what kind of resources would be allocated to theseworkloads. This computation is based on predeﬁned rules forvariable workloads (if product is of type X, then QA is done withO. Morariu et al. / Computers in Industry 67 (2015) 27–3732 workload W) and predeﬁned thresholds for ﬁxed workloads (ifproduct batch size >10, then allocate 1 additional core to thescheduler workload). The next activity provisions the detailedproduction scheduling workload. This high specs workloadcomputes the operation sequencing and resource allocationfor the whole product batch horizon. As this computation cantake a long time for large product batches with many operationsper product, the BPEL process waits asynchronously until theentire schedule is computed and dispatched via the MSB. At thispoint the detailed production scheduling workload is de-provisioned, as it is not required anymore for the productbatch execution. The resources associated to this workload arereleased to the pool. The next step is represented by theprovisioning of the production execution and quality assuranceworkloads. The process then waits for the execution tocomplete, and de-provisions the remaining workloads, releas-ing the resources to the private cloud resource pool.Section II: Event_Management consists of a while loop that waitsfor generated

The virtualizationplatform is powered by 14 blade servers with two Intel processorswith 6 cores each at 2.8 GHz and 12 Mb L3 cache. The installedmemory is 72 GB for each blade. The estimated capacity is in thearea of 400 concurrent virtual machines, considering an optimumscheduling with mixed CPU/IO proﬁles. For this pilot implementa-tion, only 4 blades were used for MES virtualization, isolated fromthe rest of the load. This was required in order to assure accuratemeasurement of message propagation between shop ﬂoor devicesand MES workloads.The virtualization process was performed in several steps. Theﬁrst step consisted in the virtualization of the MSB component thatrepresents the communication platform for all MES workloads. Thestrategy is to use two virtual network adapters, one dedicated formanagement operations, speciﬁcally the integration with the ISA-95 level 4 systems, and the other for direct Ethernet connectionwith the other MES workloads. The virtual machine was allocatedwith two virtual cores and 2 GB RAM.The second step involved the virtualization of the detailedproduction scheduler and the automatic control module, residingon a physical xSeries 3500 Server. Due to the system design thedecision was taken to split the workload in two virtual machines,one dealing with the detailed production scheduling in hierarchi-cal mode and one with the heterarchical operating module(automatic control) implemented with multi agent system insidethe MSB architecture. This approach decouples the two systemsthat used to share the same physical server and so improves theoverall reliability of the system. The third step involved thevirtualization of the static workloads: production tracking, humanintervention and quality assurance workloads for several producttypes. The virtualization process resulted in 11 individual virtualmachine templates, published in the service catalogue of the IBMCloudBurst private cloud; one for each ISA-95 function and oneadditional for the MSB implementation.A typical workload provisioning across four individual bladesusing the vMES process is illustrated in Fig. 6.  it is expected that the MES workloadswould have a higher and variable provisioning time.The scenario where a perturbation affects the system,preventing the initial schedule to be executed, is illustrated inFig. 7. Conclusions and future workThis paper introduces the vMES virtualization as a solution toreduce operational costs and improve the ﬂexibility, agility,reconﬁgurability and maintainability of the manufacturing
 sys-tem. The MES functions are mapped to speciﬁc workloads that aredeﬁned in terms of resource requirements and scaling proﬁles.Virtualization brings many advantages on the manufacturingsystem reliability too by allowing full system snapshots andbackups and quick recovery in case of failures, as well as providingbuilt-in redundancy. Most private cloud implementations offerthese features by default and can be directly adopted. MESworkload virtualization allows a separation or decoupling betweenthe physical resources and the controlling information system. Themost important advantage introduced by decoupling is thepossibility to have multiple versions of the MES workload withdifferent conﬁgurations and switch between them as required.This reconﬁguring capability is very useful when initial conﬁgura-tion or changes need to be tested and implemented on theproduction line. With virtualized MES workloads a redundancymechanism with active/active or active/passive workloads can beimplemented. In traditional implementations of manufacturingcells, implementing a MES component redundancy involvesthe use of a separate physical server, and so it induces a highoperational overhead. However, without controller redundancy itis impossible to assure high availability for MES components. Aprivate cloud implementation would be able to detect a workloadfailure and switch to the redundant workload in a very short time,assuring a high uptime for the entire MES environment.Adoption of virtualization and cloud related functionality,
 likeautomatic provision and de-provision of workloads and predictiveresource allocation represent the ﬁrst steps for implementinghighly ﬂexible manufacturing
 systems designed on SOA conceptsand patterns. In the recent years a great deal of effort has beeninvested in these cloud platforms by both academia and cloudvendors.
