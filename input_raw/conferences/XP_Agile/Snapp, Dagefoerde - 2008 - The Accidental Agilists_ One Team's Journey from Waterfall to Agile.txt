Abstract 
 
This case study outlines one team™s shift from the 
traditional waterfall methodology to an agile appro
ach

transformation occurred by accident over the course

University. Using the five stages of grieving as a

denial that the waterfall approach was failing to 
acceptance that agile practices would be the best w

the entire team re-envisioned itself, transformed i

business practices, and has evolved into a signific

2. The Accidental Transition to Agile 
 
The project timeline began to unravel during the

When the project started deviating from the ﬁperfec

Denial: ﬁI can™t believe this is happening!ﬂ
 
Early on

·
 
The advising processes were undocumented, 
very complicated, and only a handful of the 
advisors knew them well.  
·
 
Some members of the core team did not feel

·
 
Key people were not able to give this project

·
 
Some team members had never participated in

TSO™s web developer and key member of the

The response to these issues was to deny their

·
 
After a lengthy interviewing process, TSO™s

·
 
There were huge gaps in understanding of

How could TSO start designing a solution until the 
requirements documentation had been delivered? 
 
Bargaining: ﬁThings will improve if only we try

In late July 2006, the TSO team was still

3. Lessons Learned 
 
With OSASC running smoothly, TSO had time to 

reflect on the project. The team wanted to determin
e 
why the end result worked so well when the waterfal
l 
process completely fell apart. The authors™ researc
h 
brought them to the agile Manifesto and its four ke
y 
values [2]: 

 1.
 Individuals and interactions over processes 

and tools  
2.
 Working software over comprehensive 

documentation  
3.
 Customer collaboration over contract 

negotiation  
4.
 Responding to change over following a plan 
 
In the authors™ opinion, it™s very likely that this
 project 
would not have delivered any business value by the 

deadline if the team had continued to force the 

waterfall approach. Fortunately for the project, 

circumstances surrounding it forced the team to ope
rate 
in ways that reflect the agile values mentioned abo
ve. 
 · The 3-member development team consisted of 

highly collaborative ﬁgeneralists.ﬂ All of them 

pitched in to get the job done. 
· Advisors helped to create all of the paper 

prototypes and became very excited when they 

started to see concrete progress. Paper 

prototypes became our ﬁubiquitous languageﬂ 

and replaced the technical requirements 

documentation.  
· Advisors thoroughly tested every interface and 

feature as it was being created. There was a 

continuous feedback loop between advisors 

and the developers as the interfaces were 

being programmed. 
· The team remained flexible with features. As 

advisors™ understanding of the software 

evolved, their requirements changed. These 

changes were welcomed and implemented. In 

fact, changes to one problematic page were 

being made literally the day before 

deployment.  
 174 Another concept the authors discovered was that of 

time boxing. In waterfall, features tend to be fixe
d and 
costs and schedules/deadlines tend to change 

(expanding, typically) as the project progresses. I
n 
agile, what remains fixed are costs and 

schedules/deadlines. This forces features to change
 
(contracting, typically) as cost and schedule limit
s are 
approached. In the case of OSASC, what was most 

fixed was the deadline which forced the team to sta
y 
focused on the highest priority features in order t
o 
deliver something of value by early September. All 

other feature requests were queued for future relea
ses. 
 
The authors agree that agile is a much better fit f
or 
higher education and non-profit environments than t
he 
waterfall approach. Budgets and deadlines in such 

organizations are almost always fixed and any 

application development work typically must be done
 
within those constraints. Furthermore, the authors 
agree 
that agile™s emphasis on emerging requirements, sho
rt 
iterations, increments of product functionality, 

collaboration and face-to-face communication, 

transparency, and responsiveness to change align ve
ry 
well with how administrative groups in higher 

education function.

 5. Conclusion 
 
Organizational change is hard. As with any ﬁloss,ﬂ
