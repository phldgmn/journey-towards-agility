 Breaking the Ice for Agile Development of Embedded Software: 
An Industry Experience ReportPeter Manhart1and Kurt Schneider1,21DaimlerChrysler Research Center, Ulm, Germany   2now: FG Software Engineering , Universität Hannover, Germany Peter.Manhart@DaimlerChrysler.com Kurt.Schneider@Inf.Uni-Hannover.de AbstractA software engineering department in a Daimler-
Chrysler business unit was highly professional at 

developing embedded software for busses and coaches. 

However, customer specific add-ons were a regular 

source of hassle. Simple as they are, those individual 
requirements have to be implemented in hours or days 
rather than weeks or months. Poor quality or late upload 

into the bus hardware would cause serious cost and 
overhead. Established software engineering methods were considered inadequate and needed to be cut short. Agile methods offer guidance when quality, flexibility 
and high speed need to be reconciled. However, we did 
not adopt any full agile method, but added single agile 
practices to our “process improvement toolbox”. We 

suggested a number of classical process improvement 
activities (such as more systematic documentation and 

measurement) and combined them with agile elements 
(e.g. Test First Process). This combination seemed to 

foster acceptance of agile ideas and may help us to break 

the ice for a cautious extension of agile process 

improvement.1. Introduction The Software Process Department at DaimlerChrysler 
Research and Technology supports all business units. We 
have a more than ten-year experience in software process 

improvement: We help to establish measurement 
programs [1], carry out CMM [2] or related [3] 
improvements. In particular, we have applied SPICE (ISO 

15 504) and tailored it to our business unit needs. In many 
cases, we start from a goal-driven perspective [4] and 

apply experience-based process improvement [5], [6]. We 

consider all these approaches “classical software process 

improvement”.  
About three years ago, we started to investigate agile 
approaches as a potential new source of ingredients for 

our improvement toolbox. While we found a number of 

IT-projects that had successfully adopted and applied 

agile methods, business units that produce software to be 
embedded into our vehicles seemed more reluctant to give 

agility a try.  During the last year, a bus and coach business unit 
asked us to support improving a particular software 

process variant. In premium-class busses and coaches, 

software plays a more and more important role. Although 
the business unit mastered software module development 
very well for series functionality, they felt more 

challenged by features that were added or modified by an 

individual customer – often late in the process.  
We conducted a first overview analysis, then applied a 
goal-driven approach to derive process improvement 
suggestions. We recommended seven “classical” 
improvement activities – and two agile ones. After 

management had rejected two (classical) proposals, we 

started to implement the remaining seven activities. The 

well-known agile practices of “Unit Test” and “Test 
First” were included and treated just like the classical improvement suggestions. 
We expected automated Unit Test to be a good door-
opener: At first glance, the developers already created 

formal software specifications of the unit functionalities 

as a part of their documentation. So it seemed a quick win 

with few additional overhead to automate checking of the 

code against the specifications early. This would be a 
fundamental process change but still based on concepts 
that are familiar to most developers. Once accepted, it 

could be the first step towards Test First and to an even broader adoption of agile practices [7]. We prefer a 
smooth, step-to-step approach to revolutionary change of 

development practices. In a highly competitive business 

environment, the latter could be too risky and would not 

be accepted by the business units – since so little is 

known about agile method impacts.

2. Software embedded in premium busses 
During recent years, an increasing amount of 
customer benefit of busses were implemented in electric 
and electronic components. In particular, software-based 
functions add an increasing amount of customer value to 

our vehicles. Safety-oriented functions like Automatic 

Braking Systems (ABS) or Electronic Stability Package 

(ESP) were implemented by embedded software. 

Convenience functions followed, including automated 

climate control or wipers controlled by rain sensors. For 
busses, both chances and specific risks of embedded 
software are of utmost importance. 
2.1 Technology and software in busses 
Software-based functions in busses either reside in fix 
programmed controller units or are uploaded to 

programmable controllers.  

A modern bus contains between four and six 

programmable controller units ("FPS" in Figure 1). 

Controllers are connected by a low speed CAN and offer 

the following functions: 
1.Gateway: They are communication gateways to fix 
programmed controllers at aggregate level, e.g. to the 

electronic brake control unit. 2.IO: They convert hardware signals to CAN 
Messages.3.Bus Functions: On the basis of a real time operating 
system they compute and control bus-specific 

functionality like control of doors, climate, or 

audio/video equipment. 
Customer-specific software functionality is always 
realized as bus functions, in our case taking the form of 
so-called “function plans (FUPs)” according to IEC1131-
3. When a customer requests a special functionality, a 

decision is made, whether it will be fully or partially 

implemented in software. In that case, the engineering 
department has to produce one or more new function 
plans (FUPs) within time and quality constraints.

 2.2Specifics of embedded bus&coach software 
The conditions of engineering embedded software for 
high-end busses differ from those in many other 
automotive software engineering processes: 
1.A higher degree of customer-specific functionality 
combined with greater time pressure and the 
necessity to deliver very high quality. 2.Customers buying busses do not buy off the shelf.  
Their own varying business models result in a high 

degree of individualization.  3.Resulting non-standard equipment, functions, or 
parameter sets are implemented by integrating 

different proportions of third party- and OEM-

manufactured components. 
4.Software for complex electric or electronic functions 
often runs on programmable computer controllers. In 
such a software environment, change 
seems easy. 5.Customers buying busses make a major investment. 
They demand top quality. When new requirements 
come to their mind, they expect them to be 
considered, and denial of late changes due to 

software process problems are rarely acceptable.  
All of these issues increase time pressure for the 
realization of high quality customer-specific embedded 
software. These particularities also prevent any “existing” 

solutions from other car business units from being simply 
adopted. We needed to find custom-made solutions. 
2.3  Developing software-based functions 
The process for developing customer-specific 
functions is identical to regular (series) function 
development, except for the higher time pressure and a 
more challenging requirements engineering task. Figure 2 
shows the software process for software-based functions 

that we found in our initial analysis.The engineering process starts when a technical sales representative decides to implement a customer specific 
function in software. Then he or she writes a function 
description and forwards it to the affected engineering 
department. When the software team receives an order it 

selects an engineer responsible for the desired 
functionality. The engineer schedules the software order 
according to the planned upload date. Sometimes ongoing 

work has to be interrupted to realize the function in time, 
sometimes the demanded date cannot be met at all.  
Thesoftware developer then implements the FUP 
according to the requirements by using a graphical 

programming tool named LogiCAD, documents the FUP 

by using a database front-end, and specifies a plan for 

systematically checking the units functionality manually 

on a centralized hardware configuration. Since C code is 
generated by LogiCAD, a software developer has only 
limited opportunities to verify correctness: there is just a 

simulation feature which requires substantial manual 

effort.After that, the engineeringteam manager
 checks 
FUPs and enclosed documentation and forwards them to 

the system integration department. They review the document for quality attributes and manually execute the 

FUP with according to its test plan. If review and 

execution results are positive, the function is released to 

production for upload to the bus. If any review or check 
fails (on either team or integration levels) the FUP is 

returned to the developer. Requirements

 2.4 Timely delivery When producing software for individual products like 
busses, software development is highly meshed with 

production. From the viewpoint of a production plant 

manager software is just a “part” like an engine. At a 

specific point of the assembly line the software part has to 

be ready for installation (“upload”) like any other part. A 

delay of any part causes severe economic consequences:  
Without successfully completed upload, a bus cannot 
be driven. Even within the assembly hall, it needs to be 

towed around. This is costly in itself, and such a bus 

cannot be delivered, thus causing further cost. Therefore, a software configuration will be uploaded 
regardless whether all customer-specific functions are 

ready or not. If not, a bus might first be uploaded without 

them. When those parts of the software have to be 

supplemented or exchanged later during production, 

delivery and aftersales processes get out of regular order. 

A manually coordinated process named “Special Upload” 

is processed instead. In addition to its higher cost, this 

process is less mature and bears higher risks. A high 

percentage of timely delivery of customer-specific 

functions is obviously an important goal. 
2.5 Challenges in customer-specific functions 
There are various reasons that can cause late delivery 
of a customer-specific software component: 
1.Unrealistic planning: Development, quality 
assurance, integration and release cannot be 

shortened below a certain threshold. If a change 
requests is received after the last possible date to 
complete the FUPs in time, a delay is unavoidable – 
no matter what. 
2.Expert unavailable: Writing embedded software 
requires special domain knowledge, e.g. on engines 
or door physics. When all experts are engaged in 
“more important tasks”, software gets neglected and 

will be late at upload.  3.Changing priorities: priorities may be unclear or 
ignored. Changing the production plan has a major 

impact on the orders: if priorities and sequence of 

implementation of FUPs depends on their 
delays, an order may be completed in time that could not have 
been met under a first-in-first-out policy. Such 
adaptations, however, require a dynamic view of the 

production plan – which is difficult. 4.Requirements misunderstood or changed:
 A 
software developer or a sales representative with 

vague ideas about the desired functionality may 
cause several changes and iteration cycles to clarify 

requirements. Regular software components are 

usually well defined.  Unfortunately, customer-
specific functions combine development pressure 
with rather unclear and changing requirements. 
3. Related work:  
High-speed software engineering  
The desire to speed up software development is as old 
as the observation of vague or changing requirements. 
Literature gives a good recount of the various attempts to 
solve the problem:  
•Boehm´s spiral model [8] emphasized the concept of 
risk-driven iterations. By addressing one risk at a 

time, the software system should be able to avoid 
overly lengthy breadth-first analysis phases. Since special-request FUP development is usually short 

(and often simple), there is no room for additional 
cycles.•Prototyping is a well-known approach to handling 
unclear requirements. There are different variants 
that are tuned to elicit different aspects of a new 

system [9], [10]. For the above-cited reasons (rather 
small and low-complexity FUPs), any formal 
prototyping approach would be overkill. On the other 

hand, one could consider the current practice of 

developing special-request FUP code under pressure 

as “prototyping” – but, from that perspective, 
productdevelopment would be missing! 
•Agile methods [7], [11], [12] promise to deal with 
late changes just as they do with early changes.There is merit in each of these claims, and there are many 
more approaches. However, the challenge in practice is to 

slice a new method so that it will be accepted and can be 

adopted by a business unit. In our case, the business unit 

saw little benefit in changing their principal process: it 

served them well when applied to series software 
development. We needed a convincing case if we wanted 

to suggest any drastic changes – a small subset of a 

method had to break the ice. 
Agile methods have provoked a controversy between 
“classical” and “agile” opinions. Only recently did some 

authors exhibit a merge-and-mix rather than a either-or 
attitude: Boehm [13] suggests an excellent bigger picture 
that covers agile, classical, and risk-driven concepts.  
We welcome approaches like Boehm´s or Hruschka´s 
[14]. Although very similar to our principle ideas, Boehm 
addresses the adaptation of a software development 
process (before it is instantiated), whereas we are engaged in process improvement (making rather small 

changes to an 
existing and running process). Boehm´s 
book [13] appeared about a year after our improvement 

work started, so it inspired us only “in hindsight”. We

4. Goal-driven process improvement 
Our process support group often uses SPICE (ISO 15 
504) and derived models as a basis for analysis and 
process improvement.  
4.1 GQM measurement for better acceptance However, many business units prefer a more focused 
approach; e.g. when they already have a certain 
improvement area in mind, like in the case reported here. 
We may still use the SPICE (or CMMi) checklists as one 

source of analysis, but we mainly use goal-driven 

approaches like QIP [4] and GQM  [15] (Figure 3). 

-We start by analyzing and discussing the business 
goals of the business unit. Goals are refined into a 

goal tree.  -Since we want to demonstrate the impact of our 
improvement activities, we use GQM (goal–question-

metrics [15]) as a goal-driven measurement 
approach. It is widely known both in literature, and 
in practice. -In GQM, business goals are further detailed and 
broken down to indicators and metrics that allow to 

assess the impact of any activity started. 
-On the way, so-called abstraction sheets explicitly 
capture hypotheses about the current situation, and 

expectations on how a given improvement activity 
will actually change this situation. Success of software process improvement depends on 

acceptance by both management and technical staff of a 
business unit. Engineering-style measurement is a probate 

way to win – and maintain – acceptance.

 6. Application of Agile Practices 
We focused on four principles that are promoted by 
agile methods. Their roots can be tracked back to the 
above-cited approaches (Section 3); it is a contribution of 
agile methods to rephrase and combine valuable insights 

that have been made before by others.  
6.1  Agile Practices and Improvement Activities 
Based on our analysis results, we picked principles P1 
through P4 and derived concrete improvement Activities 
A1 to A6. Additional (classical) activities were derived, 
but are beyond the scope of this paper. P1: Implement the item with the highest initial value first. The highest initial value was more timely software 
uploads. The application of this principle resulted in two 
improvement activities: 
A1: All process participants get direct access to a sorted 
list of software functions. The sorting criterion is the 
upload date; the list is dynamic and reflects changes 

in production schedules.  A2: The criterion for prioritizing FUPs in development 
and system integration is not first-in-first-out, but 
Urgent First with respect to the current planned 

upload time.  
The other principles we turned into an action were: P2:Identify discrepancies of Work Products to 
Specifications as automated as possibleP3:Identify discrepancies of  Work Products to 
Specifications as soon as possibleP4:Fix discrepancies of Work Products to 
Specifications as soon as possible As pointed out above, programmers do not only write 
FUP software, but they also specify and document it. We 

considered automated Unit Test a straight-forward 
instantiation of P2; a Test First software process would be 

ideal to meet P3. And P4 is an organizational issue. To 
further support P2-P4, the software process was restructured to enforce early avoidance of errors: A3: Start implementation by writing the test plan. 
When changes are necessary modify the plan in 
advance.A4: Keep coding until the automated unit test 
succeeds.A5: Only function that have passed developer Unit 
Tests are processed by the system integration 

department.  
A6: Eliminate the extra team manager review. 
In addition to the improvement activities described 
here, we proposed a number of classical improvements 

(e.g. strengthening documentation by writing less but 

focusing on rationale rather than description; improving 

the requirements elicitation process). Research and 

Technology was mainly supporting the introduction of 

agile practices. 6.2 Technical challenges  
Although straight-forward in concept, a successful introduction of Test First required intense technical and 
organizational preparations. Without external support (in 
this case from Research and Technology), a busy unit can 

hardly spend this start-up effort. What looks so easy in 
XP books [7] boils down to stiff technical challenges in a 

practical embedded software environment like ours: 
•In the past, FUP code was checked manually against 
semi-formal specifications by using a hardware 

configuration for simulation. The system integration 
staff could often distinguish and fix some obvious 

typos from real errors on the fly.  
•Automated testing introduced an unintended degree 
of accuracy with respect to syntax and timing.  
•The “test cases” were rather specifications written for 
humans to read, not for computers to execute: For 

example, specifying entire ranges (e.g. [1..252]) as 

input was common place; however, this easily results 

in a combinatorial explosion. Before automated 

processing the plan, a human expert pruned the 

combinatorial space by selecting critical cases from 

an analysis of the function. Now it became necessary 

to teach the developers how to write more selective 

specifications.The imagination of a Test First software process rather 

challenges developers´ mindsets: It is still very unusual 

for the developers to turn from the sequence implement-

document-test to test-implement-document.

agile elements Based on the agile principles and derived activities, 
we hoped to increase flexibility, while preserving high 

quality. The goal-oriented process had led us to pick Unit  

Test and Test First as our starting subset.  Since goal-driven process improvement should be 
complemented with measurement, we needed to express 

agile promises in a more concrete and specific way. We 

derived a number of expectations or high potential

 benefits that we associate with unit test in the particular 
environment:  
•Instead of manually setting up a simulation run, 
developers should be enabled to run a full test suite 
within seconds.  •Faster module tests at the developers´ desks would 
encourage them to do quality assurance earlier, and

•We expect to drastically reduce the number of

We identified only minor risks of introducing Unit Tests 

since risk mitigation is easy: this change can be undone at 

any point. The old process will be preserved and just gain 

a new process branch for special customer requests.  
What is interesting about our application is its role 
within a “blended” classical-agile software process 

improvement effort. It is the process in which the 

(regression) Unit Tests is used and the way we built and 

inserted the technology to support it into the running 

process which makes the subtle step from applying an 

existing test technique to improving an overall process in 

a meaningful way. 
From the point of view of test technology and 
research, Unit Test is nothing new. It resembles ordinary 

“regression test”. When it is finally executed, it 
technicallyis an instance
 of regression test. It is not the 
purpose of this paper to investigate or to describe new 
testing techniques, but to include a given testing practice 

in a different process environment in order to foster more 
agile and fast high-quality production.  Figure 5 shows 

how Unit Test and Test First were linked into a 
measurement program that was specifically designed 

using GQM: it refers to the expectations (for Unit Test: 

see above), which are again broken down into questions

We started pre-measurement a few months ago, and 
will be able to verify (or falsify!) our expectations when 

we will see past-measurement. We are currently 
spreading Unit Test as an activity in the business unit. 

The complex Unit Test tool is indispensable.  
Even pre-measurement brought a number of surprises 
when comparing the actual situation to the assumptions 

collected during GQM abstraction sheets. A selection of 
relevant results was reported in Section 5.  This paper highlights the concept of framing agile 
additions in a traditional measurement framework. We 

consider this decision an important break-through for 

agile acceptance. We highly recommend this hybrid 

approach!8. Conclusions Agile method authors often refer to administrative 
software projects. There are only a few indications of their application to embedded software [16]. 
However, embedded software in automotive industry 
is one of many application areas that could use more 
flexibility at higher speed and good quality. There are 

sufficient reasons to try “breaking the ice”: 
-There are many projects that could be beneficiaries, 
but no agile method can be applied as-is, due to 

specific constraints. Our case shows one practical 

example. 
-Compared to many of the technologies currently used 
in embedded software development, agile approaches 
are still in their infancy. The community does not 

know for sure how they will affect embedded 

projects. Therefore, many hesitate to “revolutionize” 

too much at a time. 
-We have described the challenges our introduction of 
Unit Test faced, and how we have resolved them. 

Automation without changing the actual description 

language was one of the important prerequisites.
