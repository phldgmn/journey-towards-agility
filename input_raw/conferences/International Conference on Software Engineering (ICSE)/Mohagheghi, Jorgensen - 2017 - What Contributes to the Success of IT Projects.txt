  AbstractŠContext.  Each year the public sector invests large 
amounts of money in the development and modifications of 
their software systems. These investments are not always 
successful and many public sector software projects fail to 
deliver the expected benefits. Goal.
 This study aims at reducing 
the waste of resources on failed software projects through 

better understanding of the success factors and challenges. 
Method. Thirty-five completed software projects in 11 
organizations in the public sector of Norway were analyzed. 

For each project, representati
ves from the project owners, 
project management and the user organization were 

interviewed. 
Results. Small and large software projects 
reported different challenges, especially related to project 

priority. Taking advantage of agile practices such as flexible 
scope and frequent delivery increased the success rate of the 
projects. Projects with time and material contracts and 
involved clients during execution
 were more successful than 
other projects. The respondents experienced that extensive 
involvement and good competence of the client, high priority of 
the project, good dialogue between client and provider and 
appliance of agile practices were main success factors. Main 
challenges were related to technical issues, project planning 
and management, transition of the product to the user 
organization, involvement and competence of the client, and 
benefit management. Conclusions. Success factors tend to focus 
on human factors, e.g., involvement, competence and 

collaboration. Challenges focus on human factors as well as 
issues of technical nature. Both aspects need to be addressed to 
enable successful and avoid failed software projects. 
Competence, client involvement
 and benefit management are 
among factors that the public sector should focus on for 

realizing client benefits. 
Keywords-software projects; success; empirical study; agile; 
contract type I.  INTRODUCTION
 Over decades, organizations have invested large sums on 
Information Technology (IT) and the budgets continue to 
grow. Naturally, they are interes
ted in knowing the return on 

these investments. Recent surveys suggest that as much as 

10-15% of all software projects are stopped before they 

delivered anything at all [4, 
5]. The importance of improving 
software development perform
ance, including avoiding failures, has led to numerous 
surveys on failure factors of 
software projects, such as those reported in [1, 2, 3, 4, 7, 8, 9, 

10]. In spite of extensive opportunities to learn from 
experience, it seems that software projects fail for very much 

the same reasons as before. 
The SMIOS research project, led by the Simula Research 
Laboratory, started in April 
2015 and includes 11 partner 
organizations in the public sector of Norway. SMIOS is the 
Norwegian acronym for «Success with IT-Projects in the 
Public Sector». The SMIOS project aims at generating and 
disseminating knowledge about 
how to increase the benefit and success rate of investments in IT-development, based on 

the experiences of partner organizations. The results reported 

in this extended abstract are from the first phase of the 

SMIOS project, where information about 35 completed 

software projects were analyzed. The focus of the study is on 

the client perspective of project success or failure.  
II. STUDY DESIGN AND 
OVERVIEW OF THE 
PROJECTS
 We asked each of the 11 partners of the SMIOS project 
to select their 2-4 last completed 
or stopped software projects 
in 2014. An emphasis on the last
 projects was aimed to avoid 
a self-selection bias. A few of the organizations did not use 

only the last projects, but selected projects they considered as 

good examples of successful and not so successful projects. 

This deviation is not likely to 
significantly affect the results 
of the study.  For each project, we performed semi-structured 
interviews with representatives from the project owners, 
project management and the us
er organization. In total 107 
interviews were conducted. We asked about the project 
characteristics, applied processes for development as well as 

project management, resource management and benefit 

management, project outcomes 
(in terms of cost, quality, 
delivered functionality and benefits), and perceived success 

factors, challenges and lessons learned. Additionally, we 

read available project documentation. For the purpose of our 

analysis, we defined a successful
 project as (also see [6]):  A successful project is a project that is assessed by the interviewees to deliver the exp
ected client benefits or more, and where none of the respondents report large or very large 

problems on the success dimensions time control, budget 

control, quality and delivered functionality.

the different roles had very

Some characteristics of the analyzed projects are:  
• The planned budget varied
 between 10 000 Euros 
and 100 million Euros. The median budget was 
around four million Euros. 
• Types of contracts used with external providers were 

fixed price (4), time and material (12), risk sharing/target price
 (16) and other (3). Some risk 
sharing contracts had lower 
and upper limits for risk 
sharing (9) while others did 
not have such limits (7). 
• All but two projects used
 incremental or agile 
development methods. The frequency of delivery to 

production varied from one delivery (13), to several 
deliveries (two to four) (13), and frequent (more than 

four) deliveries (9) during the project execution. 
III.
 SOME INTERESTING 
FINDINGS
 Using the definition of success 
and failure given before, 
25 of the projects were categorized as successful, 5 as 

problematic and 5 as very probl
ematic (including 2 projects 
that were stopped before completion). The project success 

rate of our sample is consequently approximately 70%.  
We did not find any strong
 connection between project 
size and its success. Of the 10 projects that were not 

successful, three were categorized as large, two as medium 

large and five as small. Our results however suggest that 

larger and smaller project have different challenges, e.g., 

related to priority, client involvement and access to best 

competence.  
The relation between the success rate of the projects with 
agile development and scope flexibility is: 
• Projects with agile development and fixed scope 

had a success rate of 58% (12 projects). 
• Projects with agile devel
opment and flexible scope 
had a success rate of 87% (15 projects). 
 Additionally, we realized that frequent delivery 
increases the success rate of projects. In fact, all (8) projects 

with flexible scope and frequent delivery were categorized as 

successful. The respondents were 
clear in their opinion about how applying these agile practices allowed changing the 

scope based on external changes, learning through getting high quality feedback from 
representative users, and 
achieving more client benefit.  
Fixed price projects in our sample were more 
problematic than other projects: 
o Projects with fixed price 
contracts: 38% successful 
o Project with time & material contracts: 83% 

successful  Risk sharing contracts with an upper limit for payment 
may be experienced as fixed price (and financial loss for the 

provider) in situations with 
high cost overrun. We observed 
that clients using fixed price ty
pe of contracts tend to have a 
stronger focus on low price and a lower focus on evaluating 

provider competence when selecting providers (
fixed price behavior). Additionally, projects with fixed price type of 
contracts are less likely to deliver frequently and implement 

benefit management during the project execution, which are 
practices that are mentioned by the clients as important to the 

success of the projects.   
In total, we collected over 200 responses related to 
success factors, 120 responses related to challenges and over 

100 responses related to what should be done differently in a 

similar situation (lessons learned)
. The responses were coded 
using categories, which emerged from repeated reading and 

analysis of the responses.   
For success factors, 26% of responses were related to the 
involvement of stakeh
olders with high priority and good 
dialog between the client and provider most frequently 

mentioned. 
Competent client was mentioned in 10% of the 
responses while competent provider
 was mentioned in 13% 
of them. The usage of agile practices had the highest 

frequency among the factors related to the development 

process (9%).  Involving stakeholders and competence were most 
frequently mentioned challenges.
 Additionally, planning and initiation phase were perceived as challenging for public 

sector projects, especially for larger projects (19%); for 

example poor estimation process, unrealistic scope and 

unclear requirements. Technical issues (architecture choices, 

integration, integration with Commercial-Off-The-Shelf 
products and testing) were pe
rceived by the clients as 
challenging as well (7%). Finally, benefit management is an 
area of concern with 7% of responses.  
Not surprisingly, lessons learned highlighted success factors as well as suggested solutions to overcome the 

challenges. The interviewees specially focused on early 

delivery that allows learning as well as realizing benefits, 

involvement of the stakeholders
 (including involving the 
user organization and planni
ng for the transition), right competence and better processes for managing resources, 

and improving the project management practices. 
Our sample of projects is not large enough to make 
strong claims and probably some results are biased towards 

public organizations with better than average competence. 
Nevertheless, the study highlight
s factors to be extra aware 
of as a client in the public sector. 
IV. CONLUSIONS
 Some findings of the SMIOS project are presented in this 
extended abstract while we plan to publish the detailed 
results in future. The results, 
we hope, contributes to the 
body of empirical knowledge 
on the characteristics of 
successful software projects and typical challenges. Based on 
our experience from software projects outside Norway, and 

the similarity in success and failure factors reported across 

different nations™ software proj
ects, we believe that the value 
of the findings will not be limited to Norwegian software 
projects.  ACKNOWLEDGMENT
