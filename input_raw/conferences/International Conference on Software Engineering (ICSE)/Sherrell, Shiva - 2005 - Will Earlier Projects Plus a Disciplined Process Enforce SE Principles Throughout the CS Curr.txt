 2. THE NEED FOR EARLY PROCESS 
In beginning programming courses, students are taug
ht to 
describe an algorithm using natural language or pse
udocode and

However, many students resist all notions of using

simple process, anxious to get to code as quickly a
s possible. We 
have experienced this phenomenon with both novice 

programmers and graduate students.  Students have b
een known 
to successfully   build a system using a software e
ngineering 
process in a capstone course and then later revert 
to bad habits in 
graduate courses.  We have heard comments from stud
ents such 
as ﬁthe techniques that are taught in the software 
engineering 
course are not applicable in the real world.ﬂ Furth
ermore, non-
software engineering CS faculty may be unfamiliar w
ith modern

What can be done to rectify this situation?  Studen
ts can be 
exposed to multiple software development life cycle
 models and 
processes throughout the curriculum.  Although most

engineering textbooks discuss a variety of life cyc

unless students have the opportunity to experience

At the University of Memphis, we have modified our 
curriculum 
to provide students with hands-on experience using 
several 
different process models. In CS3:Advanced Data Stru
ctures and 
Algorithms, we use a modified waterfall model with 
feedback

However, students are encouraged to code and test t
heir modules 
using an incremental, iterative approach.  Next, in

software engineering course, students are taught th

of software engineering [6] along with the Unified 
Process (UP).  
Students gain an appreciation for business phases a

t plans for a 
significant project.  The project emphasizes projec
t management, 
modeling with UML, and the use of CASE tools as opp
osed to  
implementation.  Finally students develop and imple

capstone project in their final semester using the 
Unified Process.  
At the present time, undergraduate students also ha
ve the 
opportunity to work in multidisciplinary teams usin
g agile

formatics 
course.  We also offer the graduate course Software
 Development 
Process Models, where students are exposed to a var
iety of agile

tudents 
develop a project using an agile process and compar
e their 
experiences with the Unified Process and other proc

But what about students who now take programming co
urses in 
high school?  These students are often the most res
istant to the 
use of good software engineering practices since th
ey have been 
successful in their past programming courses withou

3. THE TRI-P-LETS PROJECT 
At the University of Memphis, we are working on 
Tri-P-LETS 
(Three
 P Learning Environment for Teachers and Students) [
7].  
The aim of this project is to enhance the curriculu
m in local high 
school programming courses thereby providing studen
ts with a 
better understanding of the field of computer scien
ce including 
how good software should be developed.  Fellows maj
oring in 
computer science assist participating teachers by p
resenting 
modules that emphasize important concepts such as p
roblem-
solving, object-oriented design, teamwork, and soft
ware 
development processes. 

Students learn to build software simulations in Age
ntSheets [4] 
using a process to identify agents and their behavi
ors, providing a 
foundation for later work in object-oriented progra
mming 
languages.  Students also record the times spent on
 different tasks 
and note their programming errors on forms similar 
to those of 
PSP, but simplified to better fit the knowledge lev
el of the high 
school students.  Because students are able to buil
d meaningful 
and fun projects in AgentSheets [5], their enthusia
sm for 
programming is reinforced and the advantages of usi
ng a 
disciplined process are demonstrated.  The three P™
s: problem-
solving, programming concepts, and process applicat
ion continue 
to be emphasized by the Fellow/teacher pairs as stu
dents move 
from programming in AgentSheets to Visual Basic.Net
 to Java. 
Throughout the 
Tri-P-LETS
 project, students are given the 
opportunity to experience the benefits of pair prog
ramming and to 
work in larger team environments.  Near the end of 
each 
academic year, students participate in a two-month 
Programming 
Challenge in which they work in teams to design, im
plement, test, 
and document a significant software project.  Proje
cts are judged 
by representatives from academia and industry.  Sin
ce students in 
project 
Tri-P-LETS
 will use a process throughout the high school 
programming curriculum (up to three courses), follo
wing a 
process will hopefully become routine.
