  Abstract
ŠResearch on globally distributed software development 
(GSD) assumes the characteristics 
of the GSD projects to differ 
from collocated projects. Results of mainly qualitative studies 
describe communication, coordination, and issues related to team 
dynamics as typical problems in GSD projects. However, there is 

a lack of quantitative studies using real project data, and proven 

research instruments and methods in which collocated and 
distributed projects are compared 
systematically. The results of 
our quantitative analysis show significant differences between 
these project types concerning team dynamics, teamwork quality, 

individual satisfaction and project performance.  The results are 
consistently ﬁfavoringﬂ the collocated projects. Hypothetically, 
applying the key principles of Scrum and XP would help to 

mitigate the problems of GSD projects.  
Keywords-teamwork; trust; 
commitment; identification; 
performance; globally distributed software development  
I.  INTRODUCTION
 A distinguished body of research within SE science has 
developed around the issues that the globalization of software 

development has raised especially during the recent decades 

[1]. Industrial experience reports and qualitative studies tell a 

story of communication and coordination problems in projects 

where the development teams comprise of employees from 
different countries, time-zones and cultural backgrounds, with 
scarce possibilities to face-to-face collaboration, getting 
acquainted with each other, and team-building. Disciplined use 
of processes and frequent visits have been suggested as good 

practices [2] [3] in globally distributed development projects 

(GSD) which is mainly motivated
 by the promise for increased 
project efficiency by utilizing the lower-cost pool of software 
professionals available globally in countries like India, China, 
and Eastern Europe [4].  
GSD research has been highlighting the role of 
communication and trust [5] [6] [7] in software projects. In 
addition, increasing attention have been drawn also to the other 
team dynamical and social factors that may well be negatively 

affected by the limited physical presence that the distributed 
development teams need to cope 
with [8]. As the sum of the 
various factors that increase the level of complexity of GSD 
projects, it is also a believed that they are more prone to failure 
that collocated projects.  
However, taking in account the topicality of GSD issues, 
there are extremely few quan
titative analyses comparing 
globally distributed and collocated industrial software projects 

and rigorously discussing on the 
issues that differentiate GSD 
projects from collocated projects. The GSD research has been 
mainly descriptive and explorative, and relied on qualitative 
research methods such as case studies [1]. 
In this paper we present te
ntative results from a study, 
where collocated and globally distributed projects were 
compared by measuring the levels team dynamics, teamwork 
quality, and individual satisfaction among the employees in the 

projects. Employees in distributed and collocated projects were 
also compared on the grounds of the characteristics of their 
work and their views on the performance level of the project 

they worked in.  
Our paper sharpens the understanding of the differences 
between the collocated and distributed software development 
projects. This understanding is fundamental for all GSD 
research. By using the esta
blished quantitative survey 
instruments that have been developed in social and 
organizational sciences, method that is underutilized in GSD 
research, our paper has also strong methodological 

contribution.

A. Work Design 
The rationale for measuring the characteristics of work in 
GSD projects is based on the way how the global employee 

resources are utilized in software projects. At least in part of 
the appeal of global distribution in software development is 
based on strategies, in which the employee resources 

responsible of the tasks that are by their nature more routine, 
repetitive, and requiring relatively lower level of competence 
and work experience, are sought from countries where pool of 

software professionals with sufficient education and 
competences is available with significantly lower costs than in 
Western Europe and North America. Thus, from this 
perspective is it reasonable to ask, if (and what kind of) 

differences there is between the employees at onsite and offsite 
with respect to their roles, responsibilities, and the qualitative 
nature of work in software development projects, and what 
kind of consequences these possible differences may have . For 

example, Durnell Cramton and Hind™s [9] model of the 
subgroup dynamics in internatio
nally distributed teams suggest 
that clear and overlapping differences in roles, responsibilities 
and status between the subgroups may easily cause 
ethnocentric attitudes an conflict between the workgroups.  
To measure the work characteristics of the employees in the 
analyzed projects, we used the Work Design Questionnaire 
(WDQ) developed by Morgeson and Humphrey [10]. WDQ 

was developed to integrate the key facets from the large 
amount of earlier measures for characteristics of work, and it 
provides a general measure for work design with high validity 

(see the original article). WDQ 
is an instrument consisting of 
total of 21 sum variables in four categories (task 
characteristics, knowledge characteristics, social 

characteristics, work context). Each sum variable consists of 3-
6 items. In our data collection 11 of these 21 variables were 
represented. However, we modified these variables by in most 

cases using only one or two items for each variable. 
B. Team Dynamics 
Trust
. Measures of team dynamics included measures for 
team trust, project commitment, and team identification. Trust 
is repeatedly named as crucial factor for successful 
collaboration in distributed software teams. Certain level of 

trust between people is needed to enable collaboration (sharing 
of work related and personal information, sharing of 
responsibilities) between people. In distributed projects 
efficient collaboration is expected between people who usually 
don™t have much common personal or professional history, 

which is seen as foundation for 
development of trust (as well as 
distrust). Corporate strategies that rely on distribution and 
outsourcing at high volume may cause anxiety among the 

employees who are concerned about the changes that the 
distribution may cause to their work both at short and longer 
term [5].  
There are various definitions for trust. One of the most used 
[11] defines it as confidence that the trustee won™t use received 
resources opportunistically in a way which could cause harm 

for the trustor. The measures of trust comprise of items that 
respectively focus on the different aspects of trust, like 
benevolence, integrity, competences and predictability (see 

[12] for review of the features and suitability of the different 
trust measures). In this study the team trust was measured by 
using the 16-item scale by Spreitzer and Mishra [13].  
Project commitment
. Project commitment can be defined as 
the ﬁsense of duty that the team feels to achieve the project™s 
goals and willingness to do what™s needed to make the project 
successfulﬂ [14]. Knowledge intensive occupations relying in 
individual skills and probl
em solving abilities within 
interdependent teams, like software development, a degree of 
self-direction and adoption of the general goals of the project 

and motivation to help the goal achievement is needed from the 
employees. Observed low project commitment should be 
understood as a warning sign of some problems that may cause 

a threat for the project success and outcomes. Project 
commitment was measured with a measure developed by 
Hoegl, Weinkauf, and Gemuenden [15].  
Organizational identification 
means emotional attachment 
with the organization and feeling that part of personal identity, 
the ﬁselfﬂ, is based on the organizational membership. Instead 
of the whole company, in project-organizations the most 
important target for identification can be the project team that 

works towards the shared goal. Te
am identification is seen as 
having positive influence on
 collaboration and team 
performance via more efficient communication, coordination, 

mutual support, and pro-social behavior. In distributed projects 
the level of identification and a
ttachment with the project team 
may be especially important because of the limited 
opportunities to relationship building via face-to-face contacts. 
I this paper we measured the 
team identification with scale 
developed by Mael and Ashforth [16], modified by [17]. 
According to meta-analysis by [18], the scale by Mael and 
Asforth is considered as preferable option to measure 

organizational identification. In our survey the scale was 
worded to measure the employees™ level of identification with 

the project team
. C. Teamwork Quality and Project Performance 
Software development is fundamentally team oriented 
action. Software development projects employ groups of 

professionals, who may have special roles based on their 
expertise like UI design, architecture design, or testing. 

However, the duties of the team members are highly 

connected, and tackling the variou
s complexities of software 
development requires efficient communication in the team and 
coordination of task
s [19]. To measure teamwork quality, we 
used the 
communication
, coordination
 and 
mutual support
 facets of the teamwork quality measure developed by [20].  
Hoegl and Gemuenden [20] also developed a measure for 
assessing the project performance, which builds on the 

previous conceptualizations by e.g. [21] and [22]. They 
separate the team performance and the personal success of the 
employees, the former comprising of 
effectiveness
 and 
2525 efficiency
 facets and the latter of 
work satisfaction
 and 
learning 
facets. To measure project performance, we used the 

effectiveness
 and 
learning
 facets, added with self-designed 
resourcing
 facet targeted to measure the satisfaction to the 
amount and quality of employee resources in the project.   
D. Individual Satisfaction 
To assess employees™ fulfillment at individual level, our 
questionnaire contained measures for 
job satisfaction
, individual 
team satisfaction
, and 
turnover interests
. High 
turnover in GSD projects has been stated as a problem 
especially in rapidly developing global IT sourcing hubs found 

from example in India, China and Eastern Europe. It has been 
suggested that there may be a connection between low 
commitment and high turnover in GSD projects [23]. 
We measured job satisfaction with two different measures. 
The measure by [24] measures the general job satisfaction with 
three questions. The Minnesota Satisfaction Questionnaire 
focuses on different aspects of work satisfaction, like for 

example personal recognition, advancement possibilities and 
feeling of worthwhile achievements.  A measure by [21] was 
used to measure individual satisfaction with working as part of 

the project team. To measure turnover intention a measure 
based on by Bozeman and Perrewe [25] was used. 
IV. DATA COLLECTION
 The research data were collected between 9/2009 and 
6/2010. Software executives of Finnish ICT companies were 

informed about our data collection project and invited to take 
part in case that suitable ongoing projects could be identified. 
Convenient sampling was used to get contact to potential 

companies. Our aim was to get data both from collocated and 

distributed projects. Criteria for identifying suitable project 

were the following: 
 The project is a software development project (not a 
continuous action, i.e. continuous maintenance project) 
 During the data collection, the project is at least in the 
middle in its lifecycle (not a project that has just 
recently started) 
 Based on the amount of personnel working in it, the 
size of the project should be at least moderate 
(minimum of 5 employees) 
 The project personnel should be fairly dedicated to the 
project in question  
In case that a company showed interest in taking part to 
data collection and that suitable project(s) was identified, the 

project and development team management of the given project 
were contacted and informed about our data collection. The 
project and team management was encouraged to inform also 
the development team members about data collection and 
discuss about their willingness to take part. Procedures of 

guaranteeing the confidentiality and anonymity were clarified, 
and the voluntariness of participation was emphasized for the 
employees. As an incentive to take part, written feedback 
reports of the basic results and possibility of comparison with 

the other participating projects were promised. However, the 
identity of the other participating companies and projects 
weren™t revealed either during the data collection or in the 

feedback reports.   
As a result, total of 10 software development project teams 
from 5 Finnish companies took take part to data collection. 

Four of the projects were co
llocated, meaning that in these 
projects all members of the de
velopment team worked from the 
same physical location. If 
any of the development team 
members were permanently located to some other country, the 

project was considered as intern

VI. DISCUSSION
 In general, our results showed remarkable differences 
between collocated and distributed software development 
projects. With only few exceptions, all the variables related to 
group dynamics, teamwork quality, project performance, and 

individual satisfaction significantly differed between these two 

types of projects. With all the variables showing significant 

differences, the mean values were more ﬁfavorableﬂ for the 
collocated projects.  
Our results strengthen the view of internationally 
distributed software development projects as more complex 
than collocated projects with respect to employee relations and 
collaboration dynamics, as well as project performance. The 
results of lower jobs satisfaction and higher turnover intentions 

in the distributed projects can be seen as reflecting the other 
results. In all the level of cons
istency that the results showed 
within group dynamics, teamwork quality, individual 

satisfaction, and project performance were however somewhat 
surprising.   
In our data some differences in the background variables 
may well be related to some of the other results. It is not a 

surprise that in distributed projects part of the employees were 
officially employed by some external resourcing service 
provider company. However, it is reasonable to believe that 
this share being as high as 41.8 %, it may well have an 

influence more directly to the 
development of team identity, 
project commitment, team trust and teamwork quality, and 
more indirectly also to the project performance. It seems that 

employees in distributed projects had a bit longer work history 

in SE than the employees in collocated projects. However, the 

education level of employees was a bit higher in collocated 
projects. 
VII. AGILITY AND 
GSD 
While a section of GSD research and literature has 
concentrated practically on finding out ways how to cope with 
the phenomena that seem to be 
at least partially inbuilt in to 
GSD fundamentals, like issues 
in intercultural communication 
and collaboration, and coordination of work between 
distributed team members (see systematic literature review by 
[1]), there has been less discussion in general on the relation 
between team-centric paradigm of software development and 
global distribution trend. A key question for that discussion 

would be, 
is it possible to maintain the team-centric approach 
to software development in globally distributed context?
 However, insights to that discus
sion can certai
nly be adopted 
from the discussion related to the rise of the ﬁagile methodsﬂ 

and their suitability for GSD pr
ojects. The feasibility of using 
agile methods also in GSD context has been discussed for 

example by [27] and [28].  
The core emphasis of the we
ll-known agile methods, Scrum 
and XP, is much on the internal
 dynamics of the development 
team and the principles how the 
team should work together.  
These principles include factors like self-direction, collective 
ownership, pair work, knowledge sharing and open 
communication, iterative planning and progress, reliance on 
individual competences and high motivation (see e.g. [29]). A 

way to overcome the seemingly fundamental incompatibility 
between the team-oriented paradigm of software development 
that have established mostly during the history of collocated 

development practices, and global distribution, may be found 
by rigorous application of agile methods like Scrum and XP in 
GSD projects. There is some evidence that by applying agile 
methods like Scrum and XP it is
 possible to increase the project 
performance via their inbuilt emphasis on the social factors 
[30]. Our claim is that GSD projects especially would gain 
much benefits of this effect
, as their core problems and 
challenges seem to be to a great extent social and 
communicational by their nature. 
In contrast with some earlie
r development process models, 
the principles of e.g. XP 
and Scrum contain concrete 
assumptions about the aspired work conditions and 
collaboration ﬁatmosphereﬂ within the development team. We 
also believe that the positive 
effect of the work towards 
reaching these conditions in GSD teams would have a positive 

effect also on the factors related to team dynamics, individual 
satisfaction, and project performance that we measured in this 
study. This is a question that should be studied empirically in 

future research.
