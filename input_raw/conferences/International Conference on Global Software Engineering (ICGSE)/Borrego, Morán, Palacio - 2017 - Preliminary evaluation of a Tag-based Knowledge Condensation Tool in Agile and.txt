Abstract
—An important challenge in Agile Global Software 
Development (AGSD) is architectural knowledge vaporization, 
i.e., the loss of technical knowledge due to a lack of 
documentation. In a previous work we identified that technical 
knowledge is usually available in unstructured textual 
electronic means (e.g. chat, mail, blogs, etc. – also known as 
UTEMs) used by AGSD workers during development, 
although not necessarily easily accessible. Also, we proposed 
the concept of Knowledge Condensation as a means to recover 
knowledge from UTEMs, by introducing a structure to their 
contents along with means to retrieve knowledge. In this work 
we present our first step towa
rds a Knowledge Condensation 
tool, through a prototype tagging mechanism to structure 
architectural knowledge during UTEM interactions.  We 
evaluated the prototype and obtained the following results: (1) 
a high perception of usefulness and a high projected intention 
of use; and (2) the identification of 6 tagging behavior profiles 
from participants. These tagging behaviors can be grouped as 
(i) participants who tag as expected, (ii) participants who tag 
more than expected, and (ii) participants who tag less than 
expected. These results suggest that it is important to cope with 
“little or no tagging” and “excessive tagging” behaviors to 
implement knowledge condensation based on UTEM tagging. 
Furthermore, we identified that tagging could be a good option 
to structure UTEM interactions, and to retrieve architectural 
knowledge from UTEM logs. Future work includes improving 
the tagging mechanism prototype based on the participants’ 
suggestions and overall evaluation results, and implementing a 
searcher prototype to further evaluate the concept of 
knowledge condensation based on tagging of UTEM 
interactions. 
Keywords- agile global software development; architectural 
knowledge; unstructured and textual electronic media; 
knowledge vaporization, knowledge condensing. 
I.
 
 
I
NTRODUCTION
 
Nowadays, the number of agile and distributed (or 
global) teams are increasing day by day. In these kind of 
teams, Agile Software Development (ASD) and Global 
Software Development (GSD) clash due to their distinct 
nature and distinct principles. On the one hand, 
ASD was 
designed for co-located teams, while teams are distributed in 
GSD. Also, in ASD knowledge sharing is preferably done 
through individuals interactions to maintain a rapid 
development [1], while in GSD is preferred to share 
knowledge by structured means (e.g. documents, wikis, 
knowledge repositories, etc.) to decrease the effect of the 
four inherent distance in this paradigm (physical, temporal, 
cultural and linguistic) [2]. These differences lead to 
problems in knowledge management 
[3], [4]. 
A highly valuable knowledge in any software 
development paradigm is relate
d to software architecture; 
from the most granular (detailed design, technical 
knowledge) to the most abstract (architectural patterns, good 
architectural practices, etc.). This kind of knowledge is 
called Architectural Knowledge (AK)
, which also includes 
design decisions and design rationale 
[5]
. 
In agile and global teams (AGSD teams) AK 
management (AKM) 
[6]
 is a challenge, due to the preference 
of personal interaction over documentation, and to time 
reduction to write proper documentation, which negatively 
affect the coding phases 
[7]. So
, in AGSD tacit knowledge 
tends to predominate over explicit knowledge 
[4], [8]
. 
AGSD conditions lead to documentation debt, which 
refers to the lack of documents in the software development 
cycle 
[9]
. AKM is affected by documentation debt when 
technical documents are missed, e.g. architecture design, 
data models, architectural rules, etc. We can say that the 
main reasons of documentation debt in AGSD are: (1) the 
agile principles which suggest to make “lighter” and 
“sufficient” documents, but the pressure to complete a sprint 
causes documentation omissions 
[10]
; (2) agile developers 
are reluctant about documentation, since they consider it as a 
secondary and non-creative activity 
[11]
. 
A consequence of documentation debt in any agile 
method is AK vaporization, i.e., losing AK caused by

. Once AK has been 
vaporized, the popular way to recover it is through source 
code analysis, which is time consuming. Further, the 
knowledge obtained is generally unstructured, incomplete, 
and inconsistent 
[13]
, which does not guarantee that the 
software evolve as it was planned at designing time. 
Analogously to the AK vaporization concept, in a 
previous work we proposed [14] the concept of AK 
condensation. This concept is based on the AGSD 
developers’ preference of using text media to interact with 
remote members, since these means (which we will call 
unstructured textual electronic media – UTEM) reduce 
communication problems caused by language differences 
[15]
. In another previous study we found that UTEM logs 
contain important AK for AGSD developers [16], but this 
knowledge is unstructured, inaccessible, dispersed and prone 
to be vaporized. To avoid knowledge vaporization, UTEM 
interactions have to be stored in a common and accessible 
repository, and there should be a classification mechanism to 
structure the UTEM interactions; so that AGSD team 
members could find AK aided with this classification 
scheme. 
In this paper, we present a classification mechanism 
based on manual tagging (as in social networks) performed 
by developers during their UTEM interactions. This way, 
these interactions will be tagged and stored in a repository, 
so AGSD team members could search for AK based on the 
classification tags inserted. We 
also present an evaluation of 
the UTEM interactions manual tagging, where usability, 
intention of use, and tagging behavior were obtained. We 
discuss the results and finally we present our conclusions. 
II.
 
T
AGGING MECHANISM
 
Tagging has been used in Integrated Development 
Environments to find and remember information on source 
code, with good adoption by developers [17]–[20]. Also, 
auto-tagging mechanisms have been developed to relate 
emails with requirements, but on
ly with a 70% of accuracy 
[21], which could be unacceptable in a production 
environment. Thus, we think that AGSD developers could 
also adopt UTEM tagging. 
However, 
one of the characteristics of tagging in social 
media is the excessive creation of tags, known as tag 
explosion [22]. For our purposes, 
this is a problem since the 
author of the tag could be the only one who knows its exact 
meaning, i.e. interpretation difference and locality of tags 
could be present [22]. Thus, knowledge retrieval could be 
problematic if it is based on a classification scheme produced 
by a mechanism without tag creation control. 
Thus, we developed a prototype mechanism to help 
AGSD developers to tag UTEM interactions. This 
mechanism uses a list of tags that 
are defined a priori by the 
group of AGSD developers (users). All tags must be related 
to a set of meta-tags that are embedded in the tagging 
mechanism and that cannot be edited or deleted. The set of 
meta-tags is based on the results of a previous empirical 
study, where we analyzed a set 
of UTEM interactions among 
AGSD developers [16]. These meta-tags represent the AK 
that AGSD developers share, according to our study (see Fig. 
1). The aim of relating custom tags and meta-tags is to 
provide a more abstract and structured way to store and find 
AK. For example, the #pythonTricks tag could be related to 
the #Code meta-tag, so that when a developer wants to 
recover AK but he does not remember the exact name of the 
tag, he could use the meta-tag to conduct a first search. 
The tagging mechanism was developed in Javascript and 
it was added to a custom in
stant messenger (IM) tool 
(developed ex professo). The ta
gging mechanism is triggered 
when the hash character and a letter are typed on a UTEM 
interface (e.g. “#a”). Then the mechanism shows a list of the 
proposed completions based on the preloaded list of tags and 
meta-tags. Then the developer selects the tag that better fits 
to the interaction topic, and the tag is inserted on the text of 
the current message (see Fig. 2). Either the mouse or the 
keyboard can be used for tag selection (using the up/down 
arrows to navigate the tag list and tab to select it). When an 
inexistent tag is entered, i.e. 
a tag that is not part of the 
preloaded list of tags and meta
-tags, the mechanism deletes 
that tag, as for the current implementation new tags cannot 
be added to the custom list through this mechanism. New 
tags are added using an administration module during Sprint 
planning or Sprint retrospective meetings.

VALUATION DESIGN
 
We conducted an evaluation to obtain the usability 
perception and the intention of use of the tagging 
mechanism. We also observed the tagging behavior of 
participants during their interactions. Participants were 30 
developers from five Mexican software development 
companies (avg. age = 27.2, std. = 3.2) with 4.7 average 
years of experience on software
 engineering (std. = 3.8), 2.3 
years of experience on ASD 
(std. = 1.1), and 1.3 average 
years of experience on Dist
ributed Software Development 
(std. = 0.8). 
The evaluation was designed as follows. Firstly, we 
explained to each pair of participants the problem of AK

Later, we asked to the participants to assume to be in a 
scenario in which both of them work for different software 
companies (A and B), and that they are collaborating in the 
same project. Company B depends of a RESTful service that 
is currently being developed by
 company A, and there is no 
actual documentation on that service yet. The developer from 
company B has to obtain the AK of the service from the 
developer from company A. With that in mind, we asked 
them to use the custom IM tool following a chatting script 
(one per participant) in separated rooms. According to the 
chatting scripts each participant has to ask three questions 
related to the RESTful service. Also, the scripts had three 
parts where we suggested them to use a tag, without 
specifying the exact tag name. With this aim, we preloaded 
four custom tags to the tagging mechanism, which were 
related to the scripts topic. These tags were: #Security, 
#ErrorHandling, #UserStory, and #BusinessRule. It is worth 
noting that we said to participants that they were free to use 
the tags, if they thought that any tag fits in the conversation. 
The complete chatting activity had an average duration of 20 
minutes.  
Once the scripts were entirely followed, participants 
answered SUS and TAM questionnaires. Finally, we asked 
participants to provide a fina
l comment or suggestion about 
the tagging mechanism.

E
VALUATION 
R
ESULTS
 
A.
 
 Tagging behavior. 
We counted the number of used tags per participant, 
distinguishing between custom tags and meta-tags, and 
identifying when they were semantically correctly used. 
Based on their behavior, we identified six different 
participant behavior profiles: 
1)
 
Expected. 
Participants who used only the three tags 
that were suggested, i.e., each 
correct tag, one 
instance per 
tag. 
2)
 
Expected + repeated. 
Participants who used the three 
suggested tags, and that repeated its use in another place in 
the conversation, i.e., each correct tag, more than one 
instance per tag. 
3)
 
Expected + meta-tags. 
Participants who used the 
three tags as suggested, and that used meta-tags in other 
places in the conversation (each correct tag, plus meta-tags, 
one or more instances per tag). 
4)
 
Less than expected. 
Participants who used less of the 
three expected tags. 
5)
 
Less than expected + repeated. 
Participants who used 
less of the three expected tags,
 and that repeated its use in 
other places in the conversation, i.e., more than one instance 
of any used tag. 
6)
 
Less than expected + meta-tags. 
Participants who 
used less of the three expected
 tags, and that used meta-tags 
in other places in the convers
ation (one or more instances 
per tag).
 
We observed that only 7 participants were classified as 
having the expected profile (see Table 1). Also, we observed 
that 17 participants used correctly more than the expected 
tag instances ((Expected + repeated) + (Expected + meta-
tags)), and only 5 
used them incorrectly. Further, 6 
participants used less than the 
expected tag instances, where 
2 of them repeated an expected instance and 3 used one 
meta-tag at least.  
We represented the tagging behavior in a bubble graph 
(see Fig. 3), where we observed 
that most of the participants 
used up to 5 tag instances. Al
so we observed an outlier case 
of 8 used tag instances. In the 
case of the tag instances used 
incorrectly, most of the cases are of only one instance.

V.
 
D
ISCUSSION 
 
One of the main findings was that the number of 
participants who used more th
an the expected tag instances 
was higher than the participants who used only the expected 
tags and less than expected. Th
is suggests that software 
engineers did not have any problems on tagging their UTEM 
interactions using the proposed mechanism. The tagging 
behavior of the participants could be reflecting the popularity 
of tagging in social systems [24], however we must explore 
better this possibility. Further, we observed that they really 
assumed their roles in the evaluation, so they were interested 
in tagging correctly to facilitate future knowledge retrieval. 
Despite the good usability perception, supported by the 
results of the SUS and TAM questionnaires, participants 
suggested a list of possible improvements, including: (1) a 
customizable way to select and navigate the completion 
suggestion list (i.e. some parti
cipants had problems using the 
TAB key to select a tag); (2) a context aware manner to 
suggest tags, i.e., based on the interaction topic; (3) include 
an additional way to see the available tags, i.e. in addition to 
autocompletion (e.g. a tag searcher, or a tree of available 
tags); (4) a mechanism to identify frequent concepts and then 
convert them into tags. These suggestions may provide 
possible explanations about the low usability perception on 
the proposed tool by 10% of the participants as identified 
using the SUS questionnaire. This 
in turn suggests that it is 
necessary to keep working on improving the usability of the 
tagging mechanism. 
 Also, it is necessary to express a word of caution 
regarding an excessive use of tags during the interactions, as 
in this evaluation we had a participant who used 8 tag 
instances instead of the 3 required. Considering a longer 
chatting period (e.g. a whole da
y), excessive tagging could 
affect the effectiveness of AK retrieval. We proposed to use 
tags to find knowledge, so if there are many similar tags in 
UTEM interactions, the search hit list could grow big, 
resulting in information overloading. In fact, after 
concluding the evaluation, about 50% of the participants 
asked for the search tool characteristics, and requested to 
consider the effectiveness of the search results, so that 
obtaining the desired knowledge in less time could be 
possible. 
Another finding was that parti
cipants used meta-tags in a 
correct manner; despite we never explained them their 
meaning. This suggests that the names proposed for these 
meta-tags are expressive, and that they relate to everyday 
situations in DSD and/or ASD. However, there are some 
meta-tags that were consistently used incorrectly (e.g. 
#TechnologicalSupport, #ArchitecturalView), and others that 
were never used (e.g. #Topic, #Generated). In the case of the 
tags that were used incorrectly, it is necessary to identify a 
more adequate name for them. Considering the meta-tags 
that were not used, we consider that the evaluation scenario 
was not suitable for the use of those tags. Thus, it is 
necessary to keep evaluating the tag set in other scenarios. 
Also we found cases were participants used less tags than 
those expected. During the evaluation, we observed that 
those participants had difficulties to select a tag, caused 
either by the selection mechanism or because they did not 
find any tag that matched the sc
ript's suggestion
; as a result 
they preferred to send the message without a tag. This 
behavior showed us that a customizable way to select tags is 
important and useful to decrease the “little or no tagging” 
cases, as well as, a mechanism to add new tags during 
interactions (thus, in-Sprints). However, we must be careful 
with the provision of a mech
anism to add new tags freely, 
because it could lead into tags explosion. 
Further, participants from this profile seemed in a hurry 
to conclude the evaluation, as they did not wanted to spend 
too much time trying to perform the activity in a correct 
manner. In real settings, ASD developers deal with similar 
in-a-hurry situations, even though this kind of situations 
cause technical debt [10]. For this reason, it is necessary to 
improve the mechanism usability 
to correctly cope with this 
kind of situations.  
Finally, the behavior of participants in the “Less than 
expected” profile, in terms of AK retrieving, could 
negatively affect knowledge acquisition, as there would be 
few (or inexistent) points to refer to when trying to find the 
required knowledge on the UTEM logs.  
VI.
 
C
ONCLUSION 
 
In this paper we presented a prototype mechanism to aid 
AGSD developers to tag UTEM interactions in order to later 
retrieve AK through them. This mechanism was evaluated by 
30 software engineers in a simulated AGSD scenario. They 
perceived the mechanism as highl
y usable and they showed a 
high intention of use. However, we received suggestions to 
improve the usability of the tagging mechanism. 
We also used the evaluation to observe the participants’ 
tagging behavior. We identified 6 participant profiles that 
can be grouped on (1) participants who tag as expected, (2)

5454 participants who tag more than 
expected, and (3) participants 
who tag less than expected. We noted that the number of 
participants who tag more than 
expected was higher than the 
others groups, which coincides with the high perception of 
usability and intention of use of participants on the tagging 
mechanism. These results suggest that tagging could be a 
good option to structure UTEM interactions, in the aim to 
retrieve AK from UTEM logs (a naturally unstructured 
source of knowledge). 
Also, we discussed about the importance to cope with 
“little or no tagging” and “excessive tagging” behaviors, as 
well as, the possibility to add new tags “on the fly”, in the 
aim to implement AK condensation based on tagging.  
As future work, we will consider the participants’ 
suggestions and the overall results of the presented 
evaluation, to improve the 
proposed tagging mechanism 
prototype. Also, we will refine the meta-tags (see Fig. 1) 
through an additional eval
uation using the improved 
prototype. Finally, we will implement a tag searcher 
prototype in order to be closer to the implementation of AK 
condensation based on tagging of UTEM interactions.
