Abstract
— This experience paper 
presents how a globally 
distributed software engineering te
am was able to deliver usable 
software at the end of each takt, wh
y this was important, and the 
benefits derived. We also des
cribe the approach taken, the 
challenges faced and the st
eps to overcome them.. 
Keywords—global software engineering; lean; usable software 
I.
 
 
I
NTRODUCTION 
 
In this experience paper we present
 an important part of our 
journey towards lean and how we successfully reached the 
important milestone of delivering 
usable software at the end of 
takt. 
A.
 
What is a takt?  
A takt is similar to a sprint. Whereas a sprint is a time-
boxed iteration that is specific to software development 
projects, a takt is fixed duration of
 time that applies to all 
departments that are engaged in th
e development of a product.  
B.
 
Importance of takts 
A takt thereby becomes th
e basis for synchronizing all 
product development activities, and ensures coordination 
across all departments involved in developing the product. 
Synchronization is important as uncoordinated activities can 
potentially result in imbalances between capacity and available 
resources. In addition, uncoordinat
ed handoffs can result in 
inefficiencies characterized by 
bottlenecks, and quality 
problems.  
II.
 
B
ACKGROUND
 
Our organization is part of a 
global team that develops 
products in a highly regulated industry. We are involved in 
developing hardware, software
, and firmware for multiple 
product families. The products must fulfill regulatory 
requirements that entail conforming to industry standards 
across all versions of the product encompassing different 
versions of firmware or third-party components. Given the 
mission-critical nature of our products, quality is non-
negotiable.  
The team had been operating for over 10 years. They had a 
defined framework to address the issues of communication, 
coordination, and control that
 are encountered in global 
software development [1]. The team also had an approach for 
effective people management [2]. 
The team switched to lean software development 
methodology to ensure on-time delivery of high-quality 
software. The drivers for transiti
oning to lean were: i) 
shortening the time between releases, ii) reduce the time spent 
in planning, iii) challenges in 
integrating sub-systems [3].  
As the project involved developing devices with hardware, 
firmware and software, there was a strong motivation to ensure 
that usable software was available at the end of each takt. It 
also was in line with the deliver as fast as possible principle of 
lean [4].  
III.
 
I
NITIAL IMPLEMENTATION OF LEAN
 
A.
 
Project organization 
The project team was distributed over four locations in 
three countries. The project team was 
split into multiple takt 
teams. While the takt teams were in different locations, 
members of a takt team were collocated. The teams used a 
common set of development 
tools across locations. 
B.
 
Takt team composition 
Each takt team typically 
had about seven to ten team 
members. A takt team brings in
 all relevant roles into a single 
self-sufficient team and hence consi
sted of cross-functional 
team members who were responsible for realizing the 
requirements [5]. The good way to 
understand a takt team is to 
consider it as the small company within the organization that 
owns a set of requirements.  
A takt team is similar to a s
crum team in that it contains 
cross functional competences. In a pure software only product 
development, it is the same as a scrum team. 
The representative of the takt team was the takt speaker, 
who was usually an individual w
ith deeper knowledge of the 
product and domain. The role of
 the takt speaker is similar to 
that of a scrum master in agile. Th
e takt speaker is responsible 
for defining the flow of require
ment realization activities, the 
interdependencies among takts and di
fferent projects, and other 
coordination tasks.  
The takt teams also had a fe
w dedicated test experts who 
were responsible for defining the test cases for the 
requirements realized in a takt and for executing these test 
cases. The tests included regression testi
ng, integration testing, 
testing of non-functional requi
rements and specific tests 
required to conform to regulations.

Nature of requirements  
Due to the high level of interdependence among the 
components, a single requi
rement spanned multiple takt teams 
that were based in different lo
cations. Though we adopted a 
takt-based approach for the im
plementation and testing 
activities, we continued managing requirements in a traditional 
way, since:  
•
 
There was a high level of dependency between the 
different software and ha
rdware components, and in 
addition the third-party modules. 
•
 
There were a large number of requirements, and most of 
them were big and complex. 
D.
 
Addressing the requirements 
From the total set of requirements, a subset was selected for 
each takt, and from this subset specific requirements were 
assigned to individuals by the takt speaker.  
To ensure the required test and delivery objectives of the 
product were fulfilled, we defined separate hardening takts 
dedicated to fixing defects and testing of the integrated product 
in the entire system. No new requirements were realized in the 
hardening takts. 
This approach did significantly 
help in increasing the speed 
and predictability in delivering software. However, we faced 
issues that hindered our ability to deliver usable software at the 
end of each takt. 
IV.
 
I
SSUES FACED IN INITIAL IMPLEMENTATION OF LEAN
 
Despite re-engineering the processes [3] we still faced 
issues, and the main objective 
to have usable software after 
every takt was not achieved. In 
addition, several requirements 
were still spanning multiple takts. 
A.
 
Development teams being spread across multiple locations 
globally 
In many cases, realizing a requirement needed the 
participation takt teams based in different locations and in 
different time zones. As a 
result, seeking and getting 
clarifications took more time, which made it difficult to meet 
the takt deadlines. 
 
In addition, we observed that team members were hesitant 
in openly voicing their opinions with team members in 
different locations. 
B.
 
Requirements prioritized over tests  
With requirements being pr
ioritized over tests, more new 
requirements were being included 
in a takt. This in turn 
required creating and executing an even larger number of tests 
within the takt. Since mo
re time was taken towards 
implementing requirements, the 
time available to complete 
tests was inadequate. Increasingly, the requirements developed 
in a takt were not tested sufficiently, and increasingly, a 
number of tests were being deferred to subsequent takts and in 
some cases even to the hard
ening takts. As a result, the defects 
in the newly realized requirements were not being detected in 
the same takt. In addition,
 due to the priority to realize 
requirements, the defects detected in a takt were not fixed in 
the same takt. On several occasions, despite requirements being 
realized, the takt deliverables were not co
mpleted, since these 
could not be tested.  
Assigning a higher priority to requirement realization also 
resulted in skewing the test effort across the takts. There was a 
much higher test load in the later takts, which resulted in undue 
pressure on the test experts to complete the testing in less time, 
which was coun
ter-productive. 
Issues related to non-functi
onal requirements were being 
uncovered mainly in the hardening 
takts. Due to 
the difficulty 
in reproducing the defects rel
ated to non-functional 
requirements, identifying the 
root causes and solutions is 
typically time and effort intensi
ve. The shortage 
of time for 
testing made it difficult to detect and resolve such issues 
effectively. Even after allotting the h
ardening takts for testing 
before release, on several occasions we still observed field 
issues in the customer environment. 
Defects detected in previous releases/versions of the 
product (legacy defects) were 
assigned a lower priority 
compared to new requirements. This resulted in legacy defects 
getting accumulated and these were increasingly being fixed in 
the hardening takts. The fixing 
of many defects in a short time 
towards the end of the project impacted quality. Since quality 
was could not be compromised, 
it introduced delays in 
delivery. 
C.
 
Delayed integration 
Since it needed multiple takts to complete development of a 
complex requirement, it was only possible to integrate the 
associated components in the later takts. In some cases, the 
integration was only possible in 
the final development takt. As 
a result, integration-related issu
es were identified towards end 
of the development takts.  
Fixing these issues needed considerable effort from all takt 
teams and resulted in a substan
tial increase in the work load 
towards the end of the proj
ect. It was difficult to address these 
issues effectively in the limited time available, which in turn 
impacted quality and delivery time. The late integration of the 
code developed by the independent 
takt teams also resulted in 
collaboration-related issues. It was not uncommon to see the 
different takt teams pointing fingers at each other with regard a 
specific issue, leading to a wast
e of time and reducing trust. 
D.
 
Increased dependency on a few key resources 
There was an increasing dependency on a few key team 
members, due to their specialized knowledge which spanned 
across multiple components. More 
requirements were being 
assigned to these key team members as it was difficult to 
distribute load am
ong team me
mbers, which resulted in new 
bottlenecks. 
E.
 
Culture and mindset of team  
As requirements were assigned by the takt speaker to team 
members, they assumed the ov
erall responsibility was with the 
takt speaker, and didn’t own the 
task in spirit. In addition, the 
team members were more focused on achieving individual 
goals by completing tasks 
assigned to them, rather than 
focusing on achieving the takt 
team goals and the overall 
project goals.  
117117 Perhaps most importantly, the team members were not 
convinced that in a single takt, 
they could meaningfully realize 
requirements, detect defects, and fix these defects.  
V.
 
A
NALYZING THE ROOT CAUSES 
 
At the end of every takt, we conduct a retrospective 
meeting where we discuss the issues faced, what went well, 
what went wrong, and what prac
tices we should continue. 
Action items are defined to address the aspects that went 
wrong. In addition, at the end of each version, we have a 
lessons learned meeting wher
e inputs from all the retrospective 
meetings are consolidated and th
e root causes for different 
issues are identified. The root 
causes we identified are listed 
below. 
•
 
Developing a single requirement 
involved takt teams in 
different locations. 
•
 
Large and complex requirements that could not be 
completed in a single takt. 
•
 
Prioritization of realizing requirements over testing and 
fixing defects (managing 
technical debt).   
•
 
Integration of components def
erred to later takts. 
•
 
Non-functional requiremen
ts not getting adequate 
focus in the early stages of realizing a requirement. 
•
 
Insufficient build and test
 automation and lack of 
scalable infrastructure.  
•
 
Insufficient knowhow sharing resulting in increased 
dependency on a few key resources. 
•
 
Culture and mindset change to ensure usable software 
at the end of each takt. 
After identifying the root causes, the takt teams proposed 
measures to address them. The teams then collectively 
evaluated the approach
es for feasibility. Eventually, a few 
measures were applied. These are described in the next section.  
VI.
 
M
EASURES TAKEN TO OVERCOME ISSUES AND THE RESULTS 
OF THE MEASURES
 
A.
 
Minimizing cross locatio
n involvement in 
developing a 
single requirement 
Knowledge transfer sessions were introduc
ed to ensure that 
the necessary competences to develop a single requirement 
were available in a single location. Where collocation of 
required competences was not possible at a single location, 
teams in different locations participat
ed in the development of 
a single requirement. To enab
le this, we institutionalized daily 
virtual standup meetings 
involving all takts in all regions. 
Fortunately this was possible due
 to favorable location 
distribution. We observed that
 the daily virtual stand up 
meetings were not effective in 
addressing the synchronization 
issues that were common in the 
globally distributed team. 
Addressing these issues invol
ved detailed discussions and 
arriving at common approaches. Hence, we institutionalized 
separate synchronization meetings at
 predefined time slots that 
provided time to discuss and resolve such issues. It was 
mandatory for all stakeholders to attend the synchronization 
meetings. 
Results: 
•
 
Single requirement realized in single location wherever 
possible. 
•
 
No synchronization issues 
B.
 
Splitting large and complex 
requirements to smaller and 
simpler requirements 
We applied the principles of user-centered design to split 
large and complex requirements in
to smaller requirements that 
could be completed with-in one 
takt. To do this we conducted 
workshops that facilitated a 
dialog among the relevant 
stakeholders (product owners, 
domain experts, architects, test 
experts, developers and usability 
experts). In the workshops the 
requirements were initially split 
into tasks, and subsequently 
into user stories [6, 7]. A task is 
an activity of a user which is 
closed and independent from other tasks. As the team is fully 
aware of the context in which the user performs the task, it 
could be translated into a user story. In this way, a single 
requirement was split into sev
eral user stories with fine-grained 
descriptions. Development of deta
iled user stories was 
completed prior to start of th
e takt and were sent for review 
across all locations to help minimize need for seeking and 
getting clarifications across locations which took more time. 
Results: 
•
 
Better clarity of the requirements and better 
understanding of the req
uirements achieved due to 
fine-grained detailing.  
•
 
Progress was transparent and clear to all stakeholders. 
The closed (completed) and open (not started) 
requirements were distinct and work in progress was 
minimized.  
•
 
Able to deliver the entire v
ersion addressed with most 
important requirements on time and with specified 
quality. 
C.
 
Prioritizing requirements effectively 
We demanded that user stories 
be defined at least two takts 
in advance. This meant that the user stories for the first and 
second takts were defined in the planning phase. As the user 
stories contained fine-grain
ed details, we were able to identify 
the interdependencies readily. Wh
ile planning a takt only user 
stories with no unresolv
ed dependencies were included. To 
ensure a requirement was completed
 in the time of a single 
takt, wherever possible, the user stori
es originating from the 
same requirement were taken up together in separate takt teams 
working in 
parallel. The co
nversations in the periodic 
synchronization meetings went a 
long way in ensuring this. 
Results: 
•
 
Delays due to dependencies were reduced. 
•
 
Most important requirements were prioritized and 
completed in the initial takts. 
118118 D.
 
Enabling early integration of components 
We sensitized the team about th
e importance of completing 
a user story before taking on 
the next user story. The mantra 
was “stop starting and start
 finishing.” In addition, we made it 
mandatory that the code satisfied
 the defined completion 
criteria, and among other things, passed all necessary unit tests, 
passed the planned automated tests (tests had to fulfill the 
defined coverage goals), and none
 of the known defects related 
to the realized requirement re
main unfixed. We also strictly 
enforced the defined release crite
ria for each component, which 
specified the number of permitted open legacy defects for each 
severity category. This helped ensure that the teams not only 
focused on completing requirements but also committed effort 
to fixing legacy issues. Furthe
r, in the common configuration 
management system, we introd
uced a takt-specific branch and 
an integration branch, in addition to the main branch to avoid 
build issues among the globa
lly distributed teams. These 
measures helped ensure that we could 
readily integrate the 
relevant changes or realized requirements and commence 
testing of the requirements realized in a takt. It also ensured 
that at every point 
in time the code on main branch was ready 
for system testing.  
Results: 
•
 
Early detection of defects aris
ing from integration of 
components. 
•
 
Sufficient time for analysis and proper fixing of 
integration-related defects. 
•
 
Wait-time to get a build fo
r system testing reduced to 
zero. 
•
 
Number of legacy defects al
ways in control due to 
strictly enforced completion criteria. 
E.
 
Ensuring focus on
 non-functional 
requirements across the 
entire duration of a takt 
To ensure defects related to 
non-functional requirements 
were detected in the takt testi
ng itself, we automated most of 
the stress and load monitoring test cases. These automated test 
cases could be executed from any locati
on. All takt teams were 
mandated to execute these automated tests prior to merging a 
realized requirement into th
e takt branch
. Typically the 
automated tests were execu
ted daily, which h
elped in detecting 
and isolating non-functional 
issues such as memory leaks 
sooner, and provided th
e opportunity to quickly fix them. 
Result: 
•
 
No delays due to non-functional issues. 
F.
 
Appropriate automation and scalable infrastructure 
To ensure we were able to simulate typical customer 
environments, we set up multiple large configurations. This 
allowed the teams to execute 
the tests on these configurations 
from the first takt onwards [8, 9]
. We automated the setup of 
different environments, which
 enabled developers to test, 
merge (with takt branch) a
nd integrate (with integration 
branch). We optimized the automated test cases to avoid 
unnecessary wait times. We also adap
ted the test suites to 
enable parallel execution. 
Though the infrastructure was 
accessible to all teams, some system tests still require 
proximity to specific equipment (e.g. when the equipment does 
not provide soft switches) and 
need to be run from a single 
location.  
Results: 
•
 
Significant reducti
on in field 
defects. 
G.
 
Systematic knowledge management to avoid bottlenecks 
due to lack of available skills 
Software development i
nvolves a significa
nt amount of 
learning [5]. We mapped the com
ponents to the individuals in 
the project, who had a deep understanding of a component. The 
team member with the deepest knowledge of a component was 
made the primary component owner. In addition, we identified 
two other team members as secondary and tertiary component 
owners. We then defined training programs for the secondary 
and tertiary component owners. Thi
s ensured that across the 
entire project we had at least three team members who could 
independently take on deve
lopment tasks related to a 
component. We also identified code experts and requirements 
experts, who in addition to thei
r primary roles, were tasked 
with leading code reviews and 
circulating comments. The 
global team has a portal where technical information related to 
the components and the product is centrally available. Each 
team member is expected to contribute to
 the portal. We also 
introduced a periodic refresher course on Lean and 
requirements engineering. 
Results: 
•
 
Avoided resource/skill bottlenecks.  
•
 
Collective expertise of team increased. 
•
 
Increased sharing of know-how and learning from each 
other.   
H.
 
Changing the culture and mindset  
To overcome cultural barriers, such as the hesitation to 
openly voice opinions, we defined an escalation matrix, which 
specified who to contact for what type of issue. For example, 
for issues related to requirements, any team member could 
contact the product owners, and do
main experts. In addition, 
any team member could ‘stop the line’ 
if they observed issues 
in quality. 
We empowered the team members in different ways [10].  
To begi
n with they could select th
e user stories they would 
work-on based on their e
xpertise. This allowed them to 
proactively evaluate the effort, seek clarifications as needed, 
and assess the dependencies before 
committing – thereby 
enhancing the ownership mindset. We
 also set team goals and 
laid greater emphasis on team achievement over individual 
achievement. As part of this, team members were encouraged 
to take up activities outside th
eir primary role. For instance, 
developers could work-on testi
ng when the testers were fully 
loaded. 
For a more complete set of in
terventions defined to change 
the culture and mindset of the team see [11].   
 
119119 Results: 
•
 
Team goals resulted in greater collaboration and in 
many cases developers willingly supported the other 
team members to complete their tasks. 
•
 
The issues raised through the escalation matrix were 
shared through the virtual stand up meetings, which 
went a long way in addressing them effectively.

VIII.
 
C
ONCLUSION
 
The new approach helped the team achieve the goal of 
delivering usable software at the end of each takt. This was a 
significant milestone in 
our journey towards lean. The 
achievement was laudable 
and appreciated within the 
organization. As the approach demands maturity within the 
team, it takes time to realize the benefits. However, it is well 
worth the effort.
