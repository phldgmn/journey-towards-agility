Abstract—this practice paper presents how a software 
engineering organization spread across three countries successfully 
transferred the knowledge of a few identified roles for a large 
mission-critical software system that had to conform to regulatory 
requirements. Multiple releases of the system have been delivered to 
customers over the 15 years it has 
been in the market. Each release 
of the product had a focus area. The competence availability for 
these focus areas was distributed. As a natural evolution of the 
globally distributed team, greater responsibility is devolved to a 
particular location, based on the availability of the competence at 
that location. Moving the incr
eased responsibility to a location, 
created a global role, which did not exist earlier. Building the new 
role required a new skill, what is unique about a global role. 
Equipping the team members in the new skill was necessary to take 
up the roles effectively and quickly. 
The first step was the identific
ation of the competence for a 
function/role, training for which may be imparted to another 
person, who will take over the function/role. This is followed by a 
process of knowledge transfer, which ensured that a person can 
take up a new global role from another location. Prioritization 
based on ease of knowledge transfer for different areas of work, 
that was found to be effective is de
scribed. This helped reduce 
possible problems that could occur due to incorrect or incomplete 
transfer of knowledge. The advantages by such knowledge transfer 
that resulted in new persons taking up global roles have outweighed 
its disadvantages. The practices described are generic and can be 
applied to any organization of similar size and complexity. 
Keywords—global role, knowledge transfer, product 
management, test management, test 
strategy, stake holder 
involvement, agile, global software engineering, regulatory 
requirements.

VI.
 
PROCESS FOR KNOWLEDGE TRANSFER
 
This section provides a process to 
initiate and complete a 
knowledge transfer from one 
location to another. Identified 
people devote between 50%-70% of 
time for training purposes. 
 
Figure 3 The process steps involved in knowledge transfer 
A.
 
Identification of the role/func
tion and its suitability for 
transfer 
The first step involves identifyi
ng the function/role that is 
suitable for knowledge 
transfer. Before initiating the transfer, 
the role gets clearly delineated with outcome expectations and 
well defined interface partners.  
B.
 
Knowledge transfer  
The persons who facilitate the knowledge transfer at source 
and destination sites are identified. The knowledge transfer is 
initiated by establishing communication between the two.  
It is possible that the persons involved visit each other’s 
location for a period that is considered necessary to facilitate an 
effective interpersonal communic
ation for the transfer of 
knowledge.  
Any competence development task that does not require 
face-to-face communication and can be trained remotely was 
done remotely by using web 
based communication tools.  
The task of knowledge transfer is considered as one of the 
overall project goals for a product release. The actual transfer 
of knowledge once initiated,
 follows the regular project 
management practices of planni
ng, tracking and reporting.  
C.
 
Overlap of roles – important step 
Once the person acquires a reasonable competence in the 
expected role, there is a period of overlap called ‘shadowing’, 
where both the persons play the role interchangeably. This 
provides required ‘on-the-job’
 training, which would facilitate 
handling the function/ro
le independently later.  
D.
 
Evalaution of independent 
functioning of the role 
Once the knowledge transfer process is completed, an 
evaluation is done, if the receiver can function independently. 
A phase, called ‘reverse shadowing’, wh
ere the giver of the 
knowledge transfer, watches over the delivery of the role by 
the receiver of knowledge transfer.  
E.
 
Complete delivery from destination – last step of the 
process 
Once it is agreed mutually that receiver person can function 
independently, the role is deliv
ered fully without any support 
from the giver of the knowledge transfer.   
The above process would complete the process steps for the 
movement of the roles.

COMPLETE 
K
NOWLEDGE TRANSFER FOR THESE ROLES MAY 
BE INCORRECT
 
This section describes the functions that have been found to 
give optimal results when not transferred completely. 
A.
 
Partial product man
agement function 
The product management has to work closely with market 
needs. If a region has several in
stallation of a product, it is 
likely that there would be a need for a faster response to the 
requirements of the customers/users there. This is best done 
within the time zone
 and in the native la
nguage, e.g like in 
Europe. Due to this reason, some people may be trained to 
perform product management functions at more than one site, 
but it is not generally recommended to
 group all of them at one 
location only.   
B.
 
Partial project management function 
Our product being a legacy product which has a large 
installed base, there is a need for project management to be 
located centrally, to give strategi
c orientation and for long term 
product goals/vision.  The pr
oject management has to work 
8484 very closely with product management to facilitate the 
realization of these goals.  This collaboration is more likely to 
succeed, in case both these functions are co-located like in our 
situation, than when they are distributed. 
C.
 
Language testing function 
For product like ours, that is av
ailable in multiple 
languages, expectation of a competence to
 test it in all those 
languages at any one site may not be justified. For example, as 
our product is released in ma
ny European languages, it is 
essential to retain language-trai
ned validation engineers at 
different sites, within Europe.  
D.
 
Customer service function 
As our product is available in
 several regions of the world, 
it became necessary to deploy customer service personnel 
closest the region 
of installation. Th
is facilitated a faster 
service, which would not be possi
ble, if these personnel were 
to travel from distant remote locati
on. This also has a monetary 
implication, when the customer service person
nel travel long 
distances, costing large amounts of money, for servicing the 
products.

OOLS USED
,
 ADVANTAGES AND DISADVANTAGES  OF A 
KNOWLEDGE TRANSFER
 
The tools used are in commercially available communicator 
tools. Novelty of the approach
 is the fit people for roles rather 
than roles fitting people. 
A.
 
Advantages/benefits  
1)
 
Talent/skill available is leveraged 
It was possible to achieve 
faster and better resolution of 
technical and management is
sues. When the group of product 
owners is co-located with the development team, they could 
evaluate and provide feedback on the product features while 
they are getting developed.  Par
ticipation of test management 
peer group in the daily stand up meetings helped align the 
testing approach.  
2)
 
Reduced dependence 
on a single site 
An effective back up was created, when technical skill of 
the people at the destination location 
took up a global role.  It is 
no longer necessary to depend on availability or time zone 
difference for a decision. Occasional work overload on a 
particular person is reduced. Due 
to availability of
 multiple 
people with adequate competence for a role at more than one 
location, any overwhelming dependence on any one site or any 
one individual may reduce. This situation is more desired as it 
can provide continuity and 
broader spread of knowledge, 
essential for a product like ours. 
3)
 
Career path at
 destination site 
It provided a career path fo
r employees who took up the 
roles, which increased the motivation and better outcomes.   
4)
 
Cost arbitage 
Though cost reduction was not 
the only goal, the transfer of 
knowledge within and across 
locations, has reduced the cost 
(measured in terms of time) of acquisition of a new employee 
by almost 50%, as compared earlier and also due to the fact 
that existing people took up
 the roles, at no extra cost.  
B.
 
Disadvantages 
1)
 
Incorrect role assignmen
t/incomplete transfer 
In such occasions, where the identification of person to 
receive the knowledge is incorrect or the knowledge transfer 
itself is ineffective, there could be
 inadequacy with consequent 
delivery of the role expectations. For example, the expectation 
of a test architect role to mentor manual and automation teams 
simultaneously. This may not
 be fulfilled, as it requires 
different types of competencies, and it may not be performed 
effectively at the same time, wit
hout adequate up-skilling of 
the test architect.  
2)
 
Remote chance of loss continuity or direction 
There is a chance of loss of continuity or direction of 
project goals, in case of differi
ng perspectives of people, if the 
person receiving the knowledge differs while collaborating 
with the person currently playi
ng the same role.  This can 
create a need for additional 
training and orientation. 
3)
 
People challenges at a remote site 
The people manager when located at a remote site can 
potentially delay or ineffectively resolve people issues. 
Resolution of people issues is 
less effective if the people 
manager is located at another site. The use of different types of 
communication tools is not a substitute for face-to-face 
interaction. There are instances, where a manager had to travel 
to another location to
 resolve such issues.
